## 1. Why This Topic Matters for EY

**Weightage:** 30–40% of the verbal section — the **single largest verbal block**. In a 12-question verbal section, expect 4–5 RC questions.

**In Pattern C (the vendor-hosted "Verbal Reasoning" block), reading comprehension is effectively the whole section**, delivered in the strict **True / False / Cannot Say** format. That format is unfamiliar to most candidates and is covered in full in Section 3.

**Why EY weights it so heavily:** the job is reading. Audit files, client submissions, regulatory guidance, management representations — a graduate's first year is spent extracting precise meaning from dense documents under time pressure. RC is the closest proxy an aptitude test has for that.

**Question styles reported:**

| Style | What it tests |
|---|---|
| **Main idea / central theme** | Global comprehension |
| **Specific detail** | Locating a stated fact |
| **Inference** | What follows but is not stated |
| **Vocabulary in context** | Meaning of a word as used |
| **Author's tone / attitude** | Register and stance |
| **Purpose of a paragraph** | Structural understanding |
| **True / False / Cannot Say** | Strict evidence discipline |

## 2. Core Concepts

### 2.1 ⭐ Read the questions first

This is the single highest-value tactic in the topic.

```
1.  Skim the QUESTIONS (not the options) — 20 seconds
2.  Read the PASSAGE with those questions in mind — 90 seconds
3.  Answer, returning to the text for each specific-detail question
```

**Why it works:** you are not studying the passage, you are **hunting** in it. Knowing what you are hunting for lets you read at speed and slow down only where it matters.

> **The exception:** for a *main idea* question, you do need the whole passage. But even then, reading the questions first tells you whether the set is detail-heavy (skim, then locate) or inference-heavy (read more carefully).

### 2.2 The four question types and how to attack each

| Type | Signal words | Method |
|---|---|---|
| **Detail** | "According to the passage…", "The author states…" | **Locate** the exact line. The answer is written down. |
| **Inference** | "It can be inferred…", "The passage suggests…" | One step beyond the text — but only one |
| **Main idea** | "The primary purpose…", "The passage is mainly about…" | Must cover the **whole** passage, not one paragraph |
| **Tone / attitude** | "The author's tone is…" | Look at adjectives and qualifiers |

### 2.3 The evidence rule

$$\boxed{\text{Every correct answer must be traceable to specific words in the passage}}$$

If you cannot point to the line that supports an option, it is wrong — however true it may be in the real world.

**The three ways a wrong option is usually built:**

| Trap type | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Too extreme** | Uses "all", "never", "only", "proves" where the passage says "many", "often", "suggests" |
| **Outside knowledge** | True in the world, but not stated in the passage |
| **Partially correct** | One clause is supported; another is not |
| **Right idea, wrong scope** | Describes one paragraph when the question asks about the whole passage |

### 2.4 Reading for structure

Most passages follow one of a few structures. Recognising the shape tells you where the main idea sits.

```
STRUCTURE A — Claim and support
    ¶1  the claim
    ¶2  evidence
    ¶3  implication
    → Main idea is in ¶1

STRUCTURE B — Problem and solution
    ¶1  the problem
    ¶2  its causes
    ¶3  proposed remedies
    → Main idea spans the whole passage

STRUCTURE C — Two views
    ¶1  view A
    ¶2  view B
    ¶3  the author's position
    → Main idea is in ¶3; the author's stance is the key

STRUCTURE D — Chronological
    ¶1  past
    ¶2  present
    ¶3  outlook
    → Main idea is the trajectory, not any single period
```

**Signal words that mark the turns:**

| Function | Words |
|---|---|
| Contrast | however · but · yet · nevertheless · on the other hand · although |
| Addition | moreover · furthermore · in addition · also |
| Consequence | therefore · thus · consequently · as a result |
| Concession | admittedly · granted · while it is true that |
| Emphasis | crucially · notably · above all · indeed |

> **The word "however" is the most important word in any passage.** It signals the author's pivot — and the sentence after it usually carries the point the question is testing.

### 2.5 Tone identification

The author's tone lives in **adjectives, adverbs and qualifiers**, not in the facts.

| Tone | Signals |
|---|---|
| **Neutral / objective** | Reports without evaluative adjectives |
| **Analytical** | Weighs evidence; uses "although", "on balance" |
| **Critical** | Negative evaluative words: flawed, inadequate, misguided |
| **Optimistic** | Promising, encouraging, significant progress |
| **Cautious** | May, might, could, suggests, appears, tentative |
| **Persuasive** | Should, must, it is essential that |

> **Most professional passages are analytical or neutral.** Options describing extreme tones — "scathing", "euphoric", "contemptuous" — are usually wrong.

## 3. The True / False / Cannot Say Format

This is the format used in EY's vendor-hosted verbal reasoning blocks (**Pattern C**), and it is where most candidates lose marks — not through poor reading, but through misunderstanding the rules.

### 3.1 The three verdicts

$$\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{TRUE} \quad&\text{The statement FOLLOWS from the passage}\\
\textbf{FALSE} \quad&\text{The statement CONTRADICTS the passage}\\
\textbf{CANNOT SAY} \quad&\text{The passage gives INSUFFICIENT information}
\end{aligned}}$$

### 3.2 ⭐ The decision procedure

```
STEP 1:  Does the passage SAY this, or logically require it?
             YES  →  TRUE

STEP 2:  Does the passage say the OPPOSITE, or rule this out?
             YES  →  FALSE

STEP 3:  Otherwise
             →  CANNOT SAY
```

**The critical discipline:** use **only** the passage. Your own knowledge — however certain — is inadmissible.

> **"Cannot Say" is correct far more often than candidates expect.** In a typical set of eight statements, three or four are usually "Cannot Say". If you are marking almost everything True or False, you are importing outside knowledge.

### 3.3 What makes a statement "Cannot Say"

| Situation | Verdict |
|---|---|
| The passage discusses A; the statement is about B | **Cannot Say** |
| The passage gives a figure for 2023; the statement is about 2024 | **Cannot Say** |
| The passage says "many companies"; the statement says "most companies" | **Cannot Say** |
| The passage explains *what* happened; the statement asserts *why* | **Cannot Say** |
| The statement is obviously true in the real world but unmentioned | **Cannot Say** |
| The passage says X *may* happen; the statement says X *will* happen | **Cannot Say** |

### 3.4 What makes a statement "False"

A statement is False only when the passage **actively contradicts** it — not merely when it fails to support it.

| Passage says | Statement | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "Revenue rose by 12%" | "Revenue fell" | **FALSE** — direct contradiction |
| "Revenue rose by 12%" | "Revenue rose by 15%" | **FALSE** — the figure conflicts |
| "Revenue rose by 12%" | "Profit rose" | **CANNOT SAY** — profit is not mentioned |
| "All branches were audited" | "Some branches were not audited" | **FALSE** |
| "Most branches were audited" | "All branches were audited" | **CANNOT SAY** |

### 3.5 The quantifier ladder

Pay close attention to how strongly the passage commits.

```
STRONGEST                                              WEAKEST
all · every  >  most  >  many  >  some  >  a few  >  may · might

A statement may only be as strong as the passage.
    Passage "some"  →  statement "all"    →  CANNOT SAY (or FALSE)
    Passage "all"   →  statement "some"   →  TRUE
```

> **Downgrading is safe; upgrading is not.** "All employees attended" ⇒ "Some employees attended" is **True**. But "Some employees attended" ⇒ "All employees attended" is **Cannot Say**.

## 4. Shortcuts & Tricks

### 4.1 The timing budget

```
For a 300-word passage with 5 questions — target 4 minutes:

    0:00 – 0:20   Skim the QUESTIONS (not the options)
    0:20 – 1:50   Read the passage, marking key lines
    1:50 – 4:00   Answer, returning to the text for detail questions
```

**Never read the passage twice in full.** Return to specific lines only.

### 4.2 Mark the passage as you read

On your rough sheet (or mentally, if the test is on-screen):

```
¶1  →  one-word summary
¶2  →  one-word summary
¶3  →  one-word summary

Circle every: however · but · therefore · although
Underline every: number · date · proper noun
```

The paragraph summaries answer main-idea questions instantly. The circled connectives mark where the author's argument turns.

### 4.3 Eliminate by extremity

```
Options containing:  all · never · only · always · none · must · proves
                     completely · entirely · impossible

…are wrong far more often than they are right, because passages
rarely commit that strongly. Check them against the text before
selecting.
```

### 4.4 The paraphrase test

Correct answers **paraphrase** the passage; wrong answers **quote it out of context** or **extend it**.

```
✅ RIGHT: says the same thing in different words
❌ WRONG: repeats the passage's words but changes the claim
❌ WRONG: adds a step the passage does not take
```

> **Be wary of an option that reuses a distinctive phrase from the passage.** Test-setters plant familiar wording in wrong options precisely because it feels reassuring.

### 4.5 For inference questions — one step only

```
The passage says:  "Costs rose faster than revenue."
✅ Valid inference: "Margins narrowed."          (one step)
❌ Over-inference:  "The company will make a loss next year."  (several steps)
```

An inference must be **necessary**, not merely plausible.

### 4.6 For main-idea questions — the scope test

```
Too NARROW  →  describes only one paragraph
Too BROAD   →  describes a topic wider than the passage
JUST RIGHT  →  covers every paragraph without adding anything
```

Test each option by asking: *"does the passage spend most of its words on this?"*

### 4.7 Sanity checks

```
✓ Can you point to the LINE that supports your answer?
✓ Does your answer rely on outside knowledge? (if so, it is wrong)
✓ Is the option's quantifier as strong as the passage's?
✓ For main idea: does it cover the WHOLE passage?
✓ For inference: is it necessary, or merely plausible?
✓ In True/False/Cannot Say: have you marked enough "Cannot Say"?
```

## 5. Solved Example Passage

**Read the following passage and answer the questions.**

> Over the past decade, corporate reporting has undergone a quiet but consequential shift. Where annual reports once confined themselves to financial statements, they now routinely include disclosures on environmental impact, workforce composition and governance practice. Investors, regulators and employees have all contributed to this expansion of scope.
>
> The change has not been uniform. Large listed companies, subject to the most direct regulatory pressure, have moved furthest; smaller firms often lack the data systems required to report reliably on non-financial matters. A 2023 survey of 400 mid-sized enterprises found that fewer than a third could produce audited emissions figures, though nearly all expressed an intention to do so within five years.
>
> Critics argue that the proliferation of reporting frameworks has produced volume without comparability. A company may report against three different standards, each defining its metrics differently, leaving readers unable to benchmark performance across firms. Efforts at harmonisation are under way, but progress has been slow, and the practical burden on preparers has in the meantime increased considerably.

---

**Q1.** What is the primary purpose of the passage?

(a) To criticise companies that fail to report emissions data
(b) To describe the expansion of corporate reporting and the difficulties it has created
(c) To advocate the adoption of a single global reporting standard
(d) To explain how investors evaluate environmental performance

**Solution.**

**Step 1 — Summarise each paragraph.**
```
¶1  →  reporting has EXPANDED beyond financials
¶2  →  the expansion is UNEVEN (large vs mid-sized firms)
¶3  →  the expansion has created PROBLEMS (no comparability)
```

**Step 2 — Apply the scope test.**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Criticise companies | ✗ **Too narrow and wrong tone** — the passage does not blame firms; it notes they lack systems |
| **(b) Describe the expansion and its difficulties** | **✓ Covers all three paragraphs** |
| (c) Advocate a single standard | ✗ The passage *mentions* harmonisation efforts but does not argue for them |
| (d) How investors evaluate | ✗ Investors appear in one clause only |

**Answer: (b)**

---

**Q2.** According to the passage, what proportion of the surveyed mid-sized enterprises could produce audited emissions figures?

(a) Nearly all
(b) About half
(c) Fewer than one third
(d) Approximately two thirds

**Solution.**

**Step 1 — Locate the line.**
> *"…found that fewer than a third could produce audited emissions figures…"*

**Step 2 — Match.**
This is a **detail question** — the answer is written down verbatim.

> **Note the trap in option (a).** "Nearly all" does appear in the same sentence, but it refers to those *intending* to report within five years, not those *able* to now. Detail questions frequently place the right figure next to the wrong subject.

**Answer: (c) Fewer than one third**

---

**Q3.** It can be inferred from the passage that the multiplicity of reporting frameworks primarily harms:

(a) Regulators seeking to impose penalties
(b) Readers attempting to compare companies
(c) Employees seeking workforce data
(d) Small firms unable to afford audits

**Solution.**

**Step 1 — Locate the relevant text.**
> *"…each defining its metrics differently, leaving **readers unable to benchmark performance across firms**."*

**Step 2 — Verify the inference is one step only.**
The passage states directly that readers cannot benchmark. Option (b) paraphrases this.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Regulators | ✗ Not mentioned in connection with the frameworks problem |
| **(b) Readers comparing companies** | **✓ Directly supported** |
| (c) Employees | ✗ Mentioned in ¶1 only, in a different connection |
| (d) Small firms and audit cost | ✗ Cost is not mentioned; the stated obstacle is **data systems** |

**Answer: (b)**

---

**Q4.** The author's tone towards the expansion of corporate reporting is best described as:

(a) Enthusiastic
(b) Dismissive
(c) Balanced
(d) Alarmed

**Solution.**

**Step 1 — Look for evaluative language.**

| Passage element | Direction |
|---|---|
| "quiet but consequential shift" | Neutral-positive |
| "The change has not been uniform" | Neutral observation |
| "Critics argue…" | Presents an opposing view without endorsing it |
| "Efforts at harmonisation are under way, but progress has been slow" | Acknowledges both sides |

**Step 2 — Assess.**
The author reports the expansion, notes its unevenness, presents criticism, and acknowledges remedial efforts alongside their limitations. No option is endorsed and no strong evaluative adjectives appear.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Enthusiastic | ✗ Too positive; the passage raises problems |
| (b) Dismissive | ✗ Too negative; the shift is called "consequential" |
| **(c) Balanced** | **✓ Both benefits and difficulties are presented** |
| (d) Alarmed | ✗ Far too strong |

**Answer: (c) Balanced**

> **Extreme tone options are almost always wrong** in professional passages. Look for the neutral or analytical option first.

---

**Q5.** The passage suggests that smaller firms lag behind larger ones principally because:

(a) They are exempt from all regulation
(b) They lack the necessary data systems
(c) Their investors are indifferent to such disclosures
(d) They report against too many frameworks

**Solution.**

**Locate the line:**
> *"…smaller firms often **lack the data systems** required to report reliably on non-financial matters."*

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Exempt from **all** regulation | ✗ The passage says large firms face "the most direct" pressure — not that small firms face none. The word "all" is too extreme. |
| **(b) Lack data systems** | **✓ Stated explicitly** |
| (c) Indifferent investors | ✗ Not stated |
| (d) Too many frameworks | ✗ That is the *critics'* complaint about all companies, not the reason small firms lag |

**Answer: (b) They lack the necessary data systems**

### 🔴 PASSAGE 5 — Questions 23–28

> The assumption that longer working hours produce more output has proved remarkably durable, despite evidence accumulated over more than a century pointing the other way. Studies from British munitions factories during the First World War found that reducing the working week from around seventy hours to around fifty left total output essentially unchanged. Similar findings have emerged repeatedly since, across industries and countries.
>
> The mechanism is not mysterious. Sustained overwork produces fatigue, and fatigue degrades both speed and accuracy. In work involving judgement rather than repetition, the accuracy penalty matters more than the speed penalty, because errors made late in a long day must be found and corrected — often by someone else, and often at greater cost than the original task.
>
> Yet the assumption persists, and the reasons are largely organisational rather than empirical. Hours are visible and easily measured; output quality frequently is not. A manager who cannot readily assess the quality of a subordinate's analysis can nonetheless observe when that subordinate leaves the office. Where measurement is difficult, effort becomes a proxy for contribution, and time spent becomes a proxy for effort.
>
> Breaking this pattern requires better measurement rather than exhortation. Organisations that have succeeded in reducing hours without losing output have generally done so by first establishing what good output looks like, and only then addressing the schedule. Attempts to reverse that order have tended to fail.

## 8. Quick Revision Sheet

> **One page. Read 48 hours after study, and again the night before the test.**

### ⭐ The method

```
1.  Skim the QUESTIONS first (not the options)      —  20 s
2.  Read the passage with those questions in mind   —  90 s
3.  Answer, returning to the text for details       — 110 s
                                              TOTAL ≈ 4 min per 5-Q passage

Mark as you read:
    ¶1 ¶2 ¶3  →  one-word summary each
    CIRCLE     →  however · but · therefore · although
    UNDERLINE  →  numbers · dates · proper nouns
```

### The four question types

```
DETAIL      "According to the passage…"   →  LOCATE the exact line
INFERENCE   "It can be inferred…"         →  ONE step beyond the text
MAIN IDEA   "The primary purpose…"        →  must cover the WHOLE passage
TONE        "The author's tone is…"       →  look at adjectives and qualifiers
```

### The evidence rule

```
Every correct answer must be traceable to specific words in the passage.
If you cannot point to the line, the option is wrong —
however true it may be in the real world.
```

### How wrong options are built

```
TOO EXTREME        all · never · only · proves · completely
OUTSIDE KNOWLEDGE  true in the world, absent from the passage
PARTIALLY CORRECT  one clause supported, another not
WRONG SCOPE        describes one ¶ when the whole passage is asked about
FAMILIAR WORDING   reuses a distinctive phrase but changes the claim
REJECTED HALF      the "not X but Y" construction — X is offered as a distractor
```

### Passage structures

```
A  CLAIM → EVIDENCE → IMPLICATION      main idea in ¶1
B  PROBLEM → CAUSE → REMEDY            main idea spans the passage
C  VIEW A → VIEW B → AUTHOR'S VIEW     main idea in the last ¶
D  PAST → PRESENT → OUTLOOK            main idea is the trajectory

"HOWEVER" is the most important word in any passage —
the sentence after it usually carries what is being tested.
```

### ⭐ TRUE / FALSE / CANNOT SAY

```
TRUE        →  the passage says it, or logically requires it
FALSE       →  the passage says the opposite, or rules it out
CANNOT SAY  →  the passage does not address it

DECISION PROCEDURE
    1.  Does the passage say / require this?      YES → TRUE
    2.  Does the passage contradict / rule out?   YES → FALSE
    3.  Otherwise                                     → CANNOT SAY

"CANNOT SAY" IS CORRECT MORE OFTEN THAN CANDIDATES EXPECT
    — typically 3 or 4 out of 8 statements.
    If you are marking almost everything True or False,
    you are importing outside knowledge.

THE QUANTIFIER LADDER
    all/every  >  most  >  many  >  some  >  a few  >  may/might

    Passage "all"  → statement "some"  →  TRUE      (downgrading is safe)
    Passage "some" → statement "all"   →  CANNOT SAY (upgrading is not)

FALSE vs CANNOT SAY
    "Revenue rose 12%" → "Revenue fell"     →  FALSE   (contradiction)
    "Revenue rose 12%" → "Profit rose"      →  CANNOT SAY (not addressed)
    "No assessment was made" → "They concluded X"  →  FALSE (denied)
```

### Tone vocabulary

```
NEUTRAL     reports without evaluation
ANALYTICAL  weighs evidence; "although", "on balance"
CRITICAL    flawed · inadequate · misguided
CAUTIOUS    may · might · suggests · appears
BALANCED    presents both benefits and difficulties

Most professional passages are NEUTRAL, ANALYTICAL or BALANCED.
Extreme options — scathing, euphoric, outraged — are almost always wrong.
```

## 9. Common Mistakes

| # | Mistake | Example | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Reading the passage before the questions** | Studying it in full first | Skim the questions in 20 seconds first |
| 2 | **Outside knowledge used** | "Parrots are birds, so…" | Use only what the passage states |
| 3 | **Extreme option accepted** | "exempt from **all** regulation" | The passage said "most direct pressure" |
| 4 | **Inference over-extended** | "Costs rose ⇒ will make a loss" | One step only: "margins narrowed" |
| 5 | **Main idea taken from one paragraph** | Choosing a ¶2 summary | It must cover the whole passage |
| 6 | **Familiar phrasing trusted** | An option reusing a distinctive phrase | Check whether the *claim* matches |
| 7 | **The rejected half chosen** | "not X but Y" ⇒ selecting X | The passage explicitly denies X |
| 8 | **Cannot Say under-used** | Marking everything True or False | 3–4 of 8 are typically Cannot Say |
| 9 | **Cannot Say over-used** | Marking a contradiction as Cannot Say | If the passage denies it, it is FALSE |
| 10 | **Quantifier upgraded** | "some" read as "all" | Downgrade is safe; upgrade is not |
| 11 | **Adjacent figure taken** | "nearly all" (intending) read as "able to now" | Match the figure to the right subject |
| 12 | **Extreme tone selected** | "outraged" for a measured passage | Prefer neutral / analytical / balanced |
| 13 | **Passage re-read in full** | Reading it twice | Return to specific lines only |
| 14 | **Blank left** | Skipping a hard inference question | No negative marking — always answer |
