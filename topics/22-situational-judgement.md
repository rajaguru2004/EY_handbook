## 1. Why This Topic Matters for EY

**This is the most under-prepared section in the entire EY assessment, and it is worth more marks than most candidates realise.**

**Weightage:** in **Pattern A**, the Situational Strength block carries **20 of roughly 56 questions — over 35% of the paper**. In **Pattern B** it is 20 of 76. In **Pattern C** it occupies a full 20-minute block equal in weight to numerical and verbal reasoning.

Candidates spend weeks on percentages worth two marks and zero minutes on a section worth twenty. That asymmetry is the single largest preparation error in EY-specific preparation.

**Why EY weights it so heavily.** Professional services is an apprenticeship business. A graduate's technical gaps can be closed in months; their judgement about when to escalate, how to handle a client's mistake, or whether to flag an inconsistency they were told to ignore cannot. The SJT is EY's attempt to assess that judgement before hiring.

**The good news:** SJTs are **highly predictable**. They test a small number of professional instincts, and once you understand what those instincts are, the scoring pattern becomes visible. This topic is learnable in one focused evening.

## 2. What the SJT Actually Measures

### 2.1 The format

You are presented with a **workplace scenario** and several possible responses. Depending on the variant, you must:

| Variant | Task |
|---|---|
| **Most / Least effective** | Pick the best response and the worst |
| **Rate each response** | Score each on a scale (Very effective → Counterproductive) |
| **Rank all responses** | Order them from best to worst |
| **Single best answer** | Choose one of four |

This handbook uses the **single best answer** format throughout, with commentary on why each alternative scores lower.

### 2.2 The competency areas being assessed

Professional-services firms publicly describe their people in terms of a consistent set of attributes. EY's own published language centres on **integrity, respect, teaming and inclusiveness**, on **energy, enthusiasm and the courage to lead**, and on **building relationships based on doing the right thing**.

Mapped onto an assessment, that produces roughly seven testable competencies:

| # | Competency | What the scenario tests |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Integrity** | Will you raise an issue that is inconvenient to raise? |
| **2** | **Client focus** | Do you protect the client's interest and the firm's relationship with them? |
| **3** | **Teaming** | Do you support colleagues, or optimise only for yourself? |
| **4** | **Prioritisation** | Can you sequence competing demands sensibly? |
| **5** | **Communication** | Do you inform the right people at the right time? |
| **6** | **Accountability** | Do you own mistakes rather than concealing them? |
| **7** | **Initiative** | Do you act within your authority without overstepping it? |

### 2.3 The persona to adopt

$$\boxed{\text{Answer as a capable first-year associate — not as a hero, and not as a passive rule-follower}}$$

**A capable first-year associate:**

```
✅ gathers facts before acting
✅ consults the right person — usually their immediate manager
✅ acts within their own authority
✅ escalates transparently when the issue exceeds that authority
✅ raises integrity concerns even when inconvenient
✅ communicates early rather than late
✅ helps colleagues without abandoning their own commitments
```

**A capable first-year associate does NOT:**

```
❌ act unilaterally on incomplete information
❌ go over their manager's head as a first step
❌ do nothing and hope the problem resolves itself
❌ conceal a mistake or hope it goes unnoticed
❌ blame a colleague to protect themselves
❌ contact the client directly without authorisation
❌ make commitments on the firm's behalf
❌ ignore a policy because it is inconvenient
```

## 3. The Decision Framework

### 3.1 ⭐ The four-step ladder

Almost every well-constructed SJT scenario has a best answer that follows this sequence:

```
        1.  GATHER INFORMATION
                understand the actual facts before responding

        2.  CONSULT the right person
                usually your immediate manager or engagement senior

        3.  ACT within your authority
                do what you are empowered to do

        4.  ESCALATE transparently if blocked
                and only after steps 1–3
```

**The best option is usually the one that sits at the correct rung of this ladder for the scenario as described.**

- If the facts are unclear ⇒ the answer involves **gathering information**
- If the decision exceeds your authority ⇒ the answer involves **consulting**
- If it is within your authority and the facts are clear ⇒ **act**
- If you have consulted and been blocked on an integrity issue ⇒ **escalate**

### 3.2 The response-quality hierarchy

Rank any set of options against this scale:

| Rank | Response type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Best** | Address the issue directly and constructively with the right person | "Raise it with the engagement manager, with the specific figures" |
| **Good** | Gather more information first, then act | "Check the source documents before flagging it" |
| **Acceptable** | Act correctly but incompletely | "Fix your own part but not mention the wider issue" |
| **Poor** | Passive — do nothing, or defer indefinitely | "Wait and see whether anyone notices" |
| **Worst** | Actively harmful — conceal, blame, or overstep | "Amend the file quietly" / "Tell the client yourself" |

### 3.3 Integrity scenarios override everything

Where a scenario involves **accuracy of a deliverable, a possible misstatement, a conflict of interest, confidentiality, or anything that could mislead a client or regulator**, the correct answer is always to **raise it** — even when:

- it is inconvenient
- a senior person told you not to worry
- it will delay the engagement
- it may embarrass a colleague

> **The one rule that never bends:** in professional services, an issue affecting the accuracy or integrity of work must be raised. There is no scenario in an SJT where the right answer is to let a known misstatement stand.

**But note the manner matters as much as the act.** The best integrity response is:
```
Raise it PRIVATELY, FACTUALLY, and with the RIGHT PERSON —
not publicly, not accusatorially, and not by going straight to the top.
```

### 3.4 The escalation sequence

```
Issue arises
     ↓
Can you resolve it yourself, within your authority?     →  YES: do it, then inform
     ↓ NO
Raise it with your IMMEDIATE manager / engagement senior
     ↓
Blocked, or the manager is the source of the concern?
     ↓ YES
Escalate to the next level — or, for ethics matters,
the designated channel (ethics hotline, independence team)
```

> **Going over your manager's head as a *first* step is almost always a wrong answer** — except where the manager is themselves the subject of the concern, or where the matter is an ethics/independence issue with a designated reporting channel.

### 3.5 Prioritisation scenarios

When facing competing deadlines, the ranking that scores best is:

```
1.  Client-facing commitments with hard external deadlines
2.  Work that BLOCKS a colleague from proceeding
3.  Internal deadlines with flexibility
4.  Self-directed or developmental work

And ALWAYS: communicate early if something will slip.
A missed deadline that was flagged three days ahead is a
management problem. The same deadline missed silently is a
trust problem.
```

## 4. Shortcuts & Tricks

### 4.1 The elimination pass

Before evaluating anything positively, **remove the options that contain these markers**:

```
❌ "…without telling anyone"
❌ "…quietly" / "…discreetly correct it yourself"
❌ "Ignore it" / "Assume someone else will notice"
❌ "Wait until someone asks"
❌ "Contact the client directly to explain"        (unless authorised)
❌ "Complain to the partner about your manager"    (as a first step)
❌ "Tell your colleague you will not help"
❌ "Refuse and explain that it is not your job"
```

In a four-option question, this typically removes two options in under ten seconds.

### 4.2 The two survivors test

You will usually be left with two plausible options. Distinguish them by asking:

```
□  Which one GATHERS INFORMATION before acting?
□  Which one involves the RIGHT PERSON?
□  Which one is PROPORTIONATE to the problem?
□  Which one KEEPS THE WORK MOVING while resolving the issue?
□  Which one would you be comfortable explaining to a partner afterwards?
```

The last question is the most reliable single test.

### 4.3 The "and" pattern

The best SJT answer frequently does **two things**: it addresses the immediate problem **and** communicates.

```
✅ "Complete the section you can, AND flag the missing data to the manager"
✅ "Help your colleague for an hour, AND tell your manager your own task may slip"
✅ "Correct the error, AND inform the reviewer so the file record is accurate"

⚠️  An option that does only ONE of these is usually second-best.
```

### 4.4 Beware the plausible extremes

| Extreme | Why it scores badly |
|---|---|
| **Total self-sacrifice** | "Work through the night alone to fix everything" — unsustainable, hides a resourcing problem |
| **Total rule-following** | "Follow the instruction exactly even though the figure is wrong" — abdicates judgement |
| **Total independence** | "Fix it yourself without telling anyone" — removes oversight |
| **Total deference** | "Do nothing since your manager said it was fine" — abdicates integrity |

**The correct answer almost always lies between these poles.**

### 4.5 Consistency matters

Many SJTs include **repeated themes across scenarios** and check whether your responses are consistent. Someone who escalates aggressively in one scenario and passively in an identical one is flagged.

```
Adopt the framework in Section 3 and apply it consistently
across all scenarios. Consistency itself is scored.
```

### 4.6 Speed discipline

You have roughly **45–60 seconds per scenario**. That is enough to:

```
0:00 – 0:15   Read the scenario; identify the competency being tested
0:15 – 0:30   Eliminate the two obviously poor options
0:30 – 0:50   Apply the two-survivors test
0:50 – 0:60   Select and move on
```

**Do not agonise.** SJT answers are rarely close once the framework is applied, and over-thinking tends to move you toward heroic or unusual responses — which score worse than the sensible ones.

## 5. Solved Examples

### Example 1 — Integrity

**Scenario.** You are working on a client engagement. While reviewing a schedule prepared by a colleague, you notice a figure that appears materially inconsistent with the underlying documentation. Your colleague is under significant time pressure and the file is due for partner review tomorrow.

**What is the most effective response?**

(a) Correct the figure yourself and say nothing, so as not to add to your colleague's pressure.
(b) Say nothing — the partner review will catch it if it is genuinely wrong.
(c) Check the underlying documentation to confirm your understanding, then raise it directly and privately with your colleague, offering to help resolve it before the review.
(d) Raise it in the team meeting so that everyone is aware of the risk.

**Step 1 — Identify the competency.**
This is an **integrity** scenario, with a **teaming** overlay. Something may be wrong with a deliverable.

**Step 2 — Eliminate.**

| Option | Marker | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | "say nothing" — conceals a change from the preparer and the reviewer | ❌ Eliminate |
| (b) | "Say nothing" — passive; relies on someone else | ❌ Eliminate |

**Step 3 — Apply the two-survivors test to (c) and (d).**

| Test | (c) | (d) |
|---|---|---|
| Gathers information first? | ✅ Checks the documentation | ❌ Raises it before verifying |
| Right person? | ✅ The colleague who prepared it | ⚠️ The whole team — disproportionate |
| Proportionate? | ✅ Private and specific | ❌ Public; embarrasses the colleague |
| Keeps work moving? | ✅ Offers to help | ⚠️ Creates discussion, not resolution |
| Comfortable explaining to a partner? | ✅ | ⚠️ |

**Answer: (c)**

> **The two features that make (c) correct:** it **verifies before raising** (you might be wrong), and it raises the issue **privately with the person best placed to fix it**. Option (d) is not wrong in substance — the issue does get raised — but the *manner* is disproportionate and damages the colleague unnecessarily.

### Example 2 — Prioritisation

**Scenario.** It is Tuesday afternoon. You have committed to delivering an internal analysis to your manager by Thursday. Your engagement senior now asks you to prepare a client deliverable due Wednesday morning. You cannot complete both to a good standard.

**What is the most effective response?**

(a) Work on the client deliverable and hope you can finish the internal analysis late on Thursday.
(b) Tell the engagement senior you already have a commitment and cannot take the client work.
(c) Immediately tell your manager and the engagement senior about the conflict, propose prioritising the client deliverable, and ask whether the internal analysis can move to Friday.
(d) Work through the night to complete both.

**Step 1 — Identify the competency.**
**Prioritisation**, with a **communication** overlay.

**Step 2 — Apply the prioritisation hierarchy.**
```
1.  Client-facing, hard external deadline   →  the Wednesday deliverable
2.  Internal deadline with flexibility      →  the Thursday analysis
```
The client work should come first — but that is only half the answer.

**Step 3 — Evaluate.**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) | Correct priority, but **fails to communicate**. The manager discovers the slip only when it happens. ⚠️ Second-best at most |
| (b) | Refuses the client work — wrong priority, and unhelpfully rigid ❌ |
| **(c)** | **Correct priority + early communication + a proposed solution** ✅ |
| (d) | Heroic self-sacrifice. Hides a genuine resourcing conflict and risks quality on both. ❌ |

**Answer: (c)**

> **The "and" pattern in action.** Option (a) gets the priority right but does only *one* of the two required things. Option (c) prioritises correctly **and** communicates **and** proposes a solution — three constructive acts rather than one.

### Example 3 — Accountability

**Scenario.** You discover that a calculation you prepared last week, which has already been shared with the client, contains an error. The error is small but it changes one of the figures in the summary the client received.

**What is the most effective response?**

(a) Correct the working file so that the calculation is right going forward, and say nothing about the version already sent.
(b) Inform your manager immediately, explain the error and its effect, and propose how to correct the position with the client.
(c) Wait to see whether the client notices; if they raise it, explain then.
(d) Email the client directly with a corrected version and an apology.

**Step 1 — Identify the competency.**
**Accountability**, with an **integrity** dimension — incorrect information has reached a client.

**Step 2 — Eliminate.**

| Option | Marker | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | "say nothing about the version already sent" — conceals | ❌ |
| (c) | "Wait to see whether the client notices" — passive concealment | ❌ |
| (d) | Contacting the client **directly** without authorisation — oversteps authority | ❌ |

**Step 3 — Confirm the survivor.**
Option (b): informs the right person, promptly, with an assessment of the impact and a proposed remedy.

**Answer: (b)**

> **Why (d) fails despite being honest.** Correcting the client is the right *outcome*, but the *route* is wrong. Client communications on an engagement go through the engagement team. A first-year associate emailing a client to correct a delivered figure bypasses review, may misstate the correction, and creates a second problem alongside the first.
>
> **Honesty is necessary but not sufficient — the channel matters too.**

### Example 4 — Teaming

**Scenario.** A colleague on your team is visibly struggling with a task you completed on a previous engagement. They have not asked for help, and you have your own deadline the same day.

**What is the most effective response?**

(a) Say nothing — they have not asked, and you have your own work.
(b) Take the task over and complete it yourself so that it is done properly.
(c) Offer briefly to share how you approached it previously, and let them decide whether they want the input.
(d) Tell your manager that your colleague is struggling.

**Step 1 — Identify the competency.**
**Teaming**, with a **respect** dimension.

**Step 2 — Evaluate each.**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) | Passive. Withholds help that costs little and could save real time ❌ |
| (b) | Overreach. Takes away their work and their learning; also risks your own deadline ❌ |
| **(c)** | **Offers help, respects their autonomy, low cost to your own commitments** ✅ |
| (d) | Escalating a colleague's difficulty before speaking to them is unnecessary and undermines them ⚠️ |

**Answer: (c)**

> **The distinguishing feature is proportionality.** The colleague is struggling, not failing; the task is not yet at risk. A brief offer of help is proportionate. Taking over (b) or escalating (d) are both responses to a more serious situation than the one described.
>
> **If the scenario had said the deadline was tomorrow and the client was at risk, (d) would move up sharply** — the right response scales with the severity described.

### Example 5 — Client interaction

**Scenario.** During a site visit, a client's finance manager asks you informally whether, in your view, their revenue-recognition treatment is acceptable. You have concerns about it but have not discussed them with your engagement team.

**What is the most effective response?**

(a) Give your honest view, since the client has asked directly and deserves a straight answer.
(b) Say you would rather not comment, and change the subject.
(c) Explain that you would prefer to review the position properly with your team before giving a view, and undertake to come back to them.
(d) Say the treatment looks fine to avoid creating alarm.

**Step 1 — Identify the competency.**
**Client focus** combined with **authority boundaries**.

**Step 2 — Eliminate.**

| Option | Marker | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Gives a firm view on a technical matter without team consultation — exceeds authority and may be wrong ❌ |
| (d) | Gives false reassurance — an integrity failure ❌ |

**Step 3 — Compare (b) and (c).**

| Test | (b) | (c) |
|---|---|---|
| Honest? | ✅ | ✅ |
| Maintains the relationship? | ❌ Evasive; leaves the client with nothing | ✅ Explains *why* and commits to follow up |
| Keeps the issue alive? | ❌ Changed the subject | ✅ Undertakes to come back |
| Comfortable explaining to a partner? | ⚠️ | ✅ |

**Answer: (c)**

> **The template for "client asks something above your level":**
> ```
> acknowledge the question  →  explain that you want to give a considered answer
>                            →  commit to coming back  →  actually come back
> ```
> This is honest, protects the firm from an unconsidered position, and leaves the client feeling taken seriously.

### Example 6 — When you have been told not to worry

**Scenario.** You raise a discrepancy in a client's inventory schedule with your engagement senior. They tell you it is immaterial and to move on. You remain concerned that it may not be immaterial.

**What is the most effective response?**

(a) Accept the senior's judgement — they have more experience and it is their call.
(b) Document your concern in the file and go directly to the partner.
(c) Ask the senior to walk you through why it is immaterial, explaining your specific concern; if you remain concerned after that, raise it with the manager.
(d) Recalculate the figure yourself and, if it is material, amend the schedule.

**Step 1 — Identify the competency.**
**Integrity** under a **hierarchy constraint** — the most discriminating type of SJT scenario.

**Step 2 — Evaluate.**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) | Deference at the expense of judgement. Experience is relevant, but "they said so" is not a resolution of your concern ⚠️ |
| (b) | Skips a rung. Going to the partner before the manager is disproportionate at this stage ❌ |
| **(c)** | **Seeks the reasoning first, then escalates one level if unresolved** ✅ |
| (d) | Acting unilaterally on the file — the recalculation may be fine, but amending without agreement removes oversight ❌ |

**Answer: (c)**

> **The two-part structure of (c) is what makes it correct.**
> **Part one** treats the senior as a colleague with reasons, not an obstacle — asking them to explain is respectful and often resolves the matter (they may have information you lack).
> **Part two** commits to escalating *one level* if the concern survives. That is the escalation ladder applied properly: consult, then escalate, and only by one rung at a time.

### GROUP A — INTEGRITY (Scenarios 1–8)

**S1.** You notice that a colleague has recorded time against a client code for work that was not performed for that client.
(a) Say nothing — it is not your responsibility.
(b) Raise it privately with the colleague first, and if it is not corrected, inform your manager.
(c) Report it immediately to the partner without speaking to the colleague.
(d) Adjust your own time entries to compensate.

**S2.** A client offers you a personal gift of significant value after a successful engagement.
(a) Accept it graciously to avoid causing offence.
(b) Politely decline, explain that firm policy restricts gifts, and inform your manager.
(c) Accept it and do not mention it to anyone.
(d) Accept it and share it with the team.

**S3.** You realise that a conclusion in a draft report is not supported by the evidence in the file, but the report has already been reviewed by two seniors.
(a) Assume the seniors know something you do not and leave it.
(b) Raise the specific point with your senior, showing exactly which evidence you believe is missing.
(c) Amend the conclusion yourself before it goes further.
(d) Note your disagreement in a personal file in case it is queried later.

**S4.** A friend outside the firm asks you about a client's forthcoming acquisition, which you know about through your work.
(a) Share only the general outline, since your friend is not in the industry.
(b) Decline to discuss it, explaining that client information is confidential.
(c) Confirm or deny what they have heard, but add nothing new.
(d) Say you cannot discuss it, but hint that something is happening.

**S5.** You are asked by a senior to backdate a document so that it appears to have been completed before a deadline.
(a) Do it — the work was substantively complete on time.
(b) Decline, explain your concern, and raise the matter with your manager if pressed.
(c) Do it, but keep a private note of the instruction.
(d) Ask a colleague to do it instead.

**S6.** During a review you find that a control you tested last year was not in fact operating, though you signed it off as effective.
(a) Leave it — it was last year's file and is now closed.
(b) Inform your manager promptly, explain what you have found, and support whatever remediation is required.
(c) Correct this year's testing and say nothing about last year.
(d) Ask the client to confirm the control was operating.

**S7.** You are offered a role on an engagement for a company in which a close family member holds a senior position.
(a) Accept — you can be objective.
(b) Disclose the relationship to your manager and the independence team before accepting.
(c) Accept but avoid working on the areas your relative is responsible for.
(d) Decline without explanation.

**S8.** A colleague asks you to sign off a workpaper you have not reviewed, saying they are confident it is correct.
(a) Sign it — you trust the colleague.
(b) Decline politely, explain that you can only sign what you have reviewed, and offer to review it if time allows.
(c) Sign it and review it afterwards.
(d) Refuse and report the request to your manager.

### GROUP B — TEAMING (Scenarios 9–16)

**S9.** A team member consistently submits work late, which delays your own tasks.
(a) Complete their work yourself to keep the engagement moving.
(b) Speak to them directly about the impact on your work and try to agree a workable schedule.
(c) Complain to the manager without speaking to the colleague.
(d) Say nothing and absorb the delays.

**S10.** During a team discussion, a quieter colleague makes a good point that is talked over.
(a) Move on — it is not your place to intervene.
(b) Return to their point explicitly, giving them credit and inviting them to expand.
(c) Repeat their point as your own to make sure it is heard.
(d) Raise it with them privately afterwards.

**S11.** A colleague takes credit in a meeting for analysis you produced.
(a) Correct them publicly in the meeting.
(b) Say nothing — the work speaks for itself.
(c) Raise it with them privately afterwards, and if it recurs, mention it to your manager.
(d) Stop sharing your work with that colleague.

**S12.** Your team is behind schedule and morale is low. You are the most junior member.
(a) Focus on your own tasks — team morale is the manager's responsibility.
(b) Complete your work reliably, offer help where you have capacity, and raise resourcing concerns constructively with your manager.
(c) Suggest to the team that everyone should work longer hours.
(d) Tell the manager the team is demoralised.

**S13.** A colleague from another team asks you for help with something outside your engagement, and you are already fully committed.
(a) Refuse and explain that you are busy.
(b) Agree, and let your own work slip.
(c) Explain your current commitments, offer a specific limited amount of help if possible, and suggest who else might assist.
(d) Ignore the request.

**S14.** You disagree with the approach your engagement senior has chosen for a piece of analysis.
(a) Carry out the work as instructed without comment.
(b) Explain your alternative approach and your reasoning, then follow the senior's decision.
(c) Do it your own way and present the result.
(d) Raise your disagreement with the manager first.

**S15.** A new joiner on your team is making basic errors that you also made when you started.
(a) Correct their work quietly each time without comment.
(b) Point out the pattern constructively and explain how you learned to avoid it.
(c) Report the errors to the manager.
(d) Leave it — they will learn from the review process.

**S16.** Two colleagues on your team are in open disagreement, and it is affecting the work.
(a) Take the side you think is correct.
(b) Stay focused on your own tasks and let them resolve it.
(c) Suggest to both, separately and neutrally, that the issue be taken to the manager for a decision.
(d) Tell the manager that the two of them cannot work together.

### GROUP C — CLIENT FOCUS (Scenarios 17–22)

**S17.** A client asks for an additional piece of analysis that is outside the agreed scope.
(a) Do the work to keep the client happy.
(b) Refuse, explaining that it is out of scope.
(c) Acknowledge the request, explain that you will confirm the scope position with your manager, and come back to them.
(d) Do the work but do not record the time.

**S18.** A client is repeatedly late in providing information you need, and the engagement timeline is at risk.
(a) Continue waiting and absorb the delay.
(b) Inform your manager promptly, with a clear list of what is outstanding and the effect on the timeline.
(c) Email the client's finance director directly to complain.
(d) Reduce the scope of your testing to fit the remaining time.

**S19.** A client contact is dismissive and unhelpful when you request documents.
(a) Escalate immediately to the partner.
(b) Remain professional, document your requests clearly in writing, and inform your manager if the position does not improve.
(c) Match their tone so they take you seriously.
(d) Stop asking and work around the gap.

**S20.** A client asks you to attend a meeting at short notice, clashing with an internal training session you are required to attend.
(a) Attend the client meeting and skip the training without telling anyone.
(b) Inform your manager of the clash and ask which takes priority.
(c) Attend the training and decline the client.
(d) Attend neither and catch up later.

**S21.** During fieldwork you identify a process improvement that would benefit the client but is outside the engagement's remit.
(a) Say nothing — it is outside scope.
(b) Mention it to the client contact directly.
(c) Raise it with your manager, who can decide whether and how to communicate it to the client.
(d) Include it in your workpapers and leave it there.

**S22.** A client complains to you about the quality of a deliverable produced by another team at the firm.
(a) Agree with the client to maintain rapport.
(b) Defend the other team without knowing the facts.
(c) Listen carefully, acknowledge the concern without judgement, and pass it promptly to your manager.
(d) Tell the client to raise it with the other team directly.

### GROUP D — PRIORITISATION & TIME MANAGEMENT (Scenarios 23–28)

**S23.** You have three tasks due today and can complete only two to a good standard.
(a) Complete all three quickly at lower quality.
(b) Identify which is most critical, inform your manager early that one will need more time, and propose a revised plan.
(c) Complete the two easiest and leave the third.
(d) Work late to finish all three.

## 7. Detailed Solutions

### GROUP A — INTEGRITY: Solutions 1–8

---

**S1. Colleague mischarging time. → (b)**

**Competency:** Integrity + proportionality.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Say nothing | ❌ Time integrity affects client billing — this cannot be ignored |
| **(b) Raise privately first, escalate if unresolved** | **✅ Proportionate, gives the colleague the chance to correct** |
| (c) Straight to the partner | ❌ Skips a rung; the colleague may have made a genuine coding error |
| (d) Adjust your own time | ❌ Compounds the problem with a second inaccuracy |

**Answer: (b)**

> **Why not (c)?** The escalation ladder applies here as elsewhere. Time-coding errors are frequently innocent mistakes. Raising it with the colleague first is both proportionate and more likely to resolve it — and (b) explicitly commits to escalating if it is not corrected.

---

**S2. Significant gift from a client. → (b)**

**Competency:** Integrity + independence.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Accept graciously | ❌ Creates an independence concern regardless of intent |
| **(b) Decline politely, cite policy, inform manager** | **✅ All three elements are needed** |
| (c) Accept and say nothing | ❌ Concealment |
| (d) Accept and share it | ❌ Sharing does not remove the independence issue |

**Answer: (b)**

> **The three elements of (b) each matter.** *Declining* addresses the substance. *Explaining that it is policy* preserves the relationship — the client is not being personally rebuffed. *Informing your manager* creates a record and lets the firm handle any follow-up.

---

**S3. Unsupported conclusion already reviewed by two seniors. → (b)**

**Competency:** Integrity under hierarchy pressure.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Assume they know better | ❌ Deference at the expense of judgement. They may have missed it precisely because it looked routine |
| **(b) Raise the specific point, showing the evidence gap** | **✅ Factual, specific, addressed to the right person** |
| (c) Amend it yourself | ❌ Unilateral change to a reviewed document |
| (d) Note it privately | ❌ Protects you but not the work — the report still goes out |

**Answer: (b)**

> **"Two seniors have already reviewed it" is the pressure being tested.** Prior review does not make an unsupported conclusion supported. The correct response is not to assert that the seniors are wrong, but to raise a **specific, evidenced point** and let them assess it.
>
> **Option (d) is the most insidious wrong answer** — it feels responsible, but documenting a concern without raising it protects only yourself.

---

**S4. Friend asks about a client's acquisition. → (b)**

**Competency:** Confidentiality.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Share the general outline | ❌ Any disclosure is a breach |
| **(b) Decline, explaining confidentiality** | **✅** |
| (c) Confirm or deny what they have heard | ❌ Confirming is itself a disclosure |
| (d) Hint that something is happening | ❌ A hint is a disclosure |

**Answer: (b)**

> **All three wrong options involve *some* disclosure.** Confidentiality is binary: confirming a rumour, sharing an outline, and hinting are all breaches. The only safe response is a clear, polite refusal that names the reason.

---

**S5. Asked to backdate a document. → (b)**

**Competency:** Integrity — the clearest case in the set.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Do it — the work was substantively complete | ❌ Backdating falsifies a record. "Substantively complete" is not a defence |
| **(b) Decline, explain, escalate if pressed** | **✅** |
| (c) Do it but keep a private note | ❌ You have still falsified the record |
| (d) Ask a colleague to do it | ❌ Transfers the wrongdoing without preventing it |

**Answer: (b)**

> **This is a bright-line scenario.** Falsifying a document date is not a judgement call, and no framing ("everyone does it", "the work was done") changes that. The correct response declines, explains **why** rather than simply refusing, and commits to escalating if pressure continues.

---

**S6. Control signed off last year was not operating. → (b)**

**Competency:** Accountability + integrity, on a closed file.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Leave it — the file is closed | ❌ A prior-year conclusion may have been wrong; that has consequences |
| **(b) Inform the manager promptly and support remediation** | **✅** |
| (c) Fix this year only | ❌ Ignores the prior-year issue |
| (d) Ask the client to confirm | ❌ Puts the client in the position of validating your own testing |

**Answer: (b)**

> **"It is last year's file" is the excuse being tested.** Discovering that a prior conclusion was wrong is uncomfortable — particularly when it was your own sign-off. But a closed file does not become correct by being closed, and the firm needs to know in order to assess the consequences.

---

**S7. Engagement where a relative holds a senior position. → (b)**

**Competency:** Independence.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Accept — you can be objective | ❌ Independence is about **appearance** as well as fact |
| **(b) Disclose to manager and independence team before accepting** | **✅** |
| (c) Accept but avoid your relative's areas | ❌ Self-designed mitigation is not the firm's to accept unilaterally |
| (d) Decline without explanation | ⚠️ Avoids the problem but gives the firm no information |

**Answer: (b)**

> **Independence issues have a designated channel** — this is one of the few areas where going outside your immediate manager is *correct*, because the independence team exists precisely for these determinations.
>
> **Note that (b) does not decline the role.** It discloses and lets the firm decide. The relationship may well be acceptable with safeguards; that is not for you to determine alone.

---

**S8. Asked to sign off a workpaper you have not reviewed. → (b)**

**Competency:** Integrity + teaming.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Sign — you trust the colleague | ❌ A sign-off asserts that *you* reviewed it |
| **(b) Decline politely, explain, offer to review if time allows** | **✅ Firm on the principle, helpful on the practicality** |
| (c) Sign then review | ❌ The assertion is false at the moment you make it |
| (d) Refuse and report the request | ❌ Disproportionate — the colleague made a request, not a demand |

**Answer: (b)**

> **The "offer to review it if time allows" clause is what elevates (b) above a bare refusal.** It upholds the principle while treating the colleague as someone with a genuine problem rather than someone making an improper request. This "decline **and** help" pattern recurs throughout well-constructed SJTs.

### GROUP B — TEAMING: Solutions 9–16

---

**S9. Colleague's lateness delays your work. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Do their work | ❌ Unsustainable; masks the problem |
| **(b) Speak to them directly about the impact; agree a schedule** | **✅** |
| (c) Complain to the manager first | ❌ Skips the direct conversation |
| (d) Absorb the delays silently | ❌ Passive; the problem recurs |

**Answer: (b)**

> **Note the framing in (b): "the impact on your work".** That is a factual, non-accusatory way to raise it. Contrast with "tell them they are always late" — same issue, adversarial framing, worse outcome.

---

**S10. Quieter colleague talked over. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Move on | ❌ A good point is lost and a colleague is diminished |
| **(b) Return to their point, credit them, invite them to expand** | **✅ Inclusive and improves the discussion** |
| (c) Repeat it as your own | ❌ Appropriation |
| (d) Raise it privately afterwards | ⚠️ Kind, but the point is still lost from the meeting |

**Answer: (b)**

> **This scenario tests inclusiveness directly.** Option (b) achieves two things at once: it recovers a useful contribution for the team, and it signals to a quieter colleague that their input carries weight. Option (d) is well-meant but only accomplishes the second.

---

**S11. Colleague takes credit for your work. → (c)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Correct them publicly | ❌ Escalates unnecessarily and looks defensive |
| (b) Say nothing | ❌ Permits a pattern to establish itself |
| **(c) Raise it privately; mention to the manager if it recurs** | **✅ Proportionate, with a defined escalation** |
| (d) Stop sharing work | ❌ Damages collaboration and does not address the behaviour |

**Answer: (c)**

> **The "if it recurs" clause is essential.** A single instance may be carelessness rather than intent. Option (c) addresses it once privately, and commits to escalation only if it becomes a pattern — which is exactly the proportionality the SJT rewards.

---

**S12. Team behind schedule, low morale, you are most junior. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Focus on yourself only | ❌ Withdraws from the team |
| **(b) Deliver reliably, help where you have capacity, raise resourcing constructively** | **✅ Three constructive actions within your authority** |
| (c) Suggest everyone work longer hours | ❌ Not yours to propose, and addresses the symptom |
| (d) Tell the manager morale is low | ⚠️ Information without a contribution |

**Answer: (b)**

> **Seniority does not remove agency.** The most junior person can still deliver reliably, help colleagues, and raise a resourcing observation. Option (b) does all three without overstepping — which is precisely the "acting within your authority" rung of the ladder.

---

**S13. Colleague from another team asks for help; you are committed. → (c)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Refuse, explaining you are busy | ⚠️ Honest but unhelpful |
| (b) Agree and let your own work slip | ❌ Breaks an existing commitment |
| **(c) Explain your position, offer limited help, suggest an alternative** | **✅ The "decline and help" pattern** |
| (d) Ignore it | ❌ |

**Answer: (c)**

> **Compare (a) and (c) carefully — they differ only in what follows the refusal.** Option (a) stops at "no". Option (c) adds a limited offer and a redirection. That addition costs almost nothing and transforms the response.

---

**S14. Disagree with your senior's chosen approach. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Do it without comment | ❌ Withholds a potentially useful view |
| **(b) Explain your reasoning, then follow the decision** | **✅ Contributes judgement, respects the decision** |
| (c) Do it your own way | ❌ Unilateral |
| (d) Go to the manager first | ❌ Skips the senior |

**Answer: (b)**

> **The second half of (b) is what makes it right.** Voicing a professional view is expected; refusing to accept the decision afterwards is not. This distinguishes a *methodology* disagreement (where the senior decides) from an *integrity* concern (where you escalate — see S3 and Example 6).

---

**S15. New joiner making basic errors. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Correct silently each time | ❌ They never learn; you absorb the work indefinitely |
| **(b) Point out the pattern constructively, share how you learned** | **✅ Teaches rather than patches** |
| (c) Report to the manager | ❌ Disproportionate for ordinary new-joiner errors |
| (d) Leave it for the review process | ❌ Wastes reviewer time and delays their learning |

**Answer: (b)**

> **"I also made this mistake when I started" is doing real work in this option.** It converts a correction into a shared experience and makes it far more likely to be absorbed. Apprenticeship models depend on this transfer happening peer-to-peer, not only through formal review.

---

**S16. Two colleagues in open disagreement affecting the work. → (c)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Take a side | ❌ Deepens the split |
| (b) Stay out of it | ⚠️ Reasonable if the work were unaffected — but it is |
| **(c) Suggest to both, separately and neutrally, that it go to the manager for a decision** | **✅ Neutral, addresses the blockage, respects both** |
| (d) Tell the manager they cannot work together | ❌ A characterisation, not a fact; damages both colleagues |

**Answer: (c)**

> **The distinction between (c) and (d).** Both involve the manager, but (c) proposes escalation *to the colleagues themselves*, neutrally, so that they raise it. Option (d) reports on them behind their backs and frames a technical disagreement as a personality problem.

### GROUP C — CLIENT FOCUS: Solutions 17–22

---

**S17. Client requests out-of-scope work. → (c)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Just do it | ❌ Scope and fee are not yours to vary |
| (b) Refuse outright | ❌ Unhelpful; damages the relationship |
| **(c) Acknowledge, confirm the scope position with your manager, come back** | **✅** |
| (d) Do it without recording time | ❌ Two problems: scope creep and inaccurate time |

**Answer: (c)**

> **The template for any out-of-scope request:** *acknowledge the request → explain you will confirm → come back promptly*. The client feels heard, the firm controls scope, and nobody is refused to their face by someone without the authority to refuse.

---

**S18. Client repeatedly late with information; timeline at risk. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Keep waiting | ❌ The risk crystallises silently |
| **(b) Inform your manager with a clear outstanding list and timeline impact** | **✅ Factual, actionable, timely** |
| (c) Email the finance director to complain | ❌ Oversteps; escalates outside the engagement channel |
| (d) Reduce your testing scope | ❌ Alters the work to fit the time — a quality failure |

**Answer: (b)**

> **Option (d) is the dangerous wrong answer** because it looks pragmatic. Quietly narrowing procedures to fit a compressed timeline is exactly the behaviour that turns a scheduling problem into a quality problem.

---

**S19. Dismissive client contact. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Escalate to the partner immediately | ❌ Disproportionate for a first difficulty |
| **(b) Stay professional, document requests in writing, inform manager if unimproved** | **✅** |
| (c) Match their tone | ❌ |
| (d) Work around the gap | ❌ Leaves an evidence gap |

**Answer: (b)**

> **"Document in writing" is the operative element.** It creates a record, removes ambiguity about what was asked and when, and gives your manager something concrete if escalation becomes necessary.

---

**S20. Client meeting clashes with mandatory training. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Skip the training without telling anyone | ❌ Unilateral, and conceals it |
| **(b) Inform your manager of the clash and ask which takes priority** | **✅** |
| (c) Decline the client | ❌ Not your call to make alone |
| (d) Attend neither | ❌ |

**Answer: (b)**

> **This is not a judgement you are equipped to make alone.** The manager knows whether the training is a regulatory requirement, whether the client meeting genuinely needs you, and what the relationship implications are. Surfacing the conflict *is* the correct action.

---

**S21. Process improvement outside scope. → (c)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Say nothing | ❌ Loses value for the client |
| (b) Tell the client directly | ❌ Bypasses the engagement team; may create scope or liability issues |
| **(c) Raise it with your manager, who decides how to communicate it** | **✅** |
| (d) Bury it in the workpapers | ❌ Technically recorded, practically lost |

**Answer: (c)**

> **Observations of value should travel up the engagement chain, not straight across to the client.** The manager can judge whether it is appropriate to raise, how to frame it, and whether it creates an opportunity or a risk.

---

**S22. Client complains about another team's work. → (c)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Agree with the client | ❌ Undermines a colleague team on the basis of one account |
| (b) Defend them without knowing the facts | ❌ Equally uninformed, and dismisses the client |
| **(c) Listen, acknowledge without judgement, pass it to your manager promptly** | **✅** |
| (d) Tell the client to raise it themselves | ❌ Deflects a legitimate concern |

**Answer: (c)**

> **"Acknowledge without judgement" is the key phrase.** You can take a complaint seriously — *"thank you for raising that, I'll make sure it reaches the right people today"* — without endorsing or rejecting it. That is the only honest position when you do not have the facts.

### GROUP D — PRIORITISATION: Solutions 23–28

---

**S23. Three tasks, capacity for two. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) All three at lower quality | ❌ Quality failure across the board |
| **(b) Identify the critical one, inform the manager early, propose a revised plan** | **✅ Prioritise + communicate + propose** |
| (c) Do the two easiest | ❌ Ease is not a priority criterion |
| (d) Work late to do all three | ⚠️ Occasionally necessary, but hides a capacity problem |

**Answer: (b)**

> **The "and" pattern again.** The critical failure in (a), (c) and (d) is not the choice of tasks — it is that **nobody is told**. Early communication converts an individual problem into a manageable one.

**S24. New task with no stated deadline. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Start immediately | ❌ May displace more urgent work |
| **(b) Ask when it is needed, then sequence** | **✅** |
| (c) Do it after the client work without checking | ⚠️ Probably right, but assumes |
| (d) Decline | ❌ |

**Answer: (b)**

> **A missing deadline is a question, not a permission.** One clarifying question takes ten seconds and removes the guesswork entirely. Option (c) will usually produce the right sequence, but it guesses where it could ask.

---

**S25. Work due at 5 p.m. will overrun; you realise mid-morning. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Say nothing and hurry | ❌ |
| **(b) Tell your manager immediately with a revised estimate and a proposal** | **✅** |
| (c) Tell them at 4:30 | ❌ Too late to act on |
| (d) Deliver what is done, without comment | ❌ |

**Answer: (b)**

> **The value of the warning is proportional to how early it comes.** At 10 a.m. the manager can reallocate, renegotiate or reduce scope. At 4:30 p.m. they can do nothing but absorb the miss. The same fact, delivered at two different times, is either useful information or a fait accompli.

---

**S26. Unfamiliar task, tight deadline. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Decline | ❌ Refuses a development opportunity |
| **(b) Accept, ask for orientation, confirm the expected output** | **✅ Willing and appropriately cautious** |
| (c) Accept and work it out alone | ❌ High risk of producing the wrong thing |
| (d) Pass it to a colleague | ❌ |

**Answer: (b)**

> **"Confirm the expected output" is often more valuable than the orientation itself.** Most wasted effort on unfamiliar tasks comes from producing the wrong deliverable rather than producing it badly.

---

**S27. Finished early on a busy engagement. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Wait to be asked | ❌ Passive on a busy engagement |
| **(b) Tell your senior you have capacity and ask what would be most useful** | **✅ Initiative within authority** |
| (c) Start something unasked | ⚠️ Well-intentioned, but may duplicate or misdirect effort |
| (d) Personal study | ❌ Not while the team is under pressure |

**Answer: (b)**

> **The distinction between (b) and (c) is the whole point of the scenario.** Both show initiative. Only (b) directs that initiative where the *team* needs it, rather than where you happen to think it is needed.

**S28. Conflicting instructions from two seniors. → (c)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Follow the more senior one | ⚠️ May be right, but leaves the other unaware |
| (b) Follow the one you agree with | ❌ Substitutes your judgement for theirs |
| **(c) Go back to both, explain the conflict, ask them to confirm** | **✅** |
| (d) Do it both ways | ❌ Wasteful; does not resolve anything |

**Answer: (c)**

> **The conflict is theirs to resolve, and they cannot resolve it if they do not know it exists.** Option (a) may produce the right work but leaves one senior with an incorrect understanding of what is being done.

### GROUP E — ACCOUNTABILITY: Solutions 29–33

---

**S29. Two days spent on the wrong analysis. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Complete it anyway | ❌ Compounds wasted effort |
| **(b) Tell your manager immediately; confirm what is required** | **✅** |
| (c) Start the correct work silently | ❌ Conceals a two-day gap that affects the plan |
| (d) Get secret help | ❌ Concealment |

**Answer: (b)**

> **Two days is a schedule fact, not just a personal embarrassment.** The manager needs it to replan. Options (c) and (d) both treat a project issue as a reputational one to be hidden.

---

**S30. Criticised for errors you accept are yours. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Explain the time pressure | ⚠️ Context has a place, but leading with it reads as excuse-making |
| **(b) Acknowledge, correct, ask what would help next time** | **✅ Ownership plus a forward orientation** |
| (c) Point out others' errors | ❌ Deflection |
| (d) Accept silently | ⚠️ Accountable but passive; nothing improves |

**Answer: (b)**

> **The third clause is what distinguishes (b) from (d).** "What would help me avoid this next time?" converts a correction into an improvement, and signals that you intend to get better rather than merely to survive the conversation.

---

**S31. Asked in a meeting about a task you have not started. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Say it is in progress | ❌ Untrue |
| **(b) State it accurately, explain why, give a realistic date** | **✅** |
| (c) Say you will check and come back | ❌ You already know the answer |
| (d) Say it is nearly complete | ❌ Untrue |

**Answer: (b)**

> **A small untruth here is more damaging than the delay itself.** Once a manager discovers that a status report was inaccurate, every subsequent report requires verification. Option (b) is uncomfortable for thirty seconds and preserves that trust.

---

**S32. Unclear whether a mistake was yours or a colleague's. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Point at the colleague | ❌ Blame before facts |
| **(b) Establish the facts, fix the problem, be open about your part as it emerges** | **✅** |
| (c) Accept responsibility immediately | ⚠️ Well-intentioned, but a false attribution helps nobody — and obscures a real process issue |
| (d) Say nothing until an investigation | ❌ Delays the fix |

**Answer: (b)**

> **Option (c) is a genuinely tempting wrong answer.** Taking the blame feels honourable. But if the error was in fact a process gap or another person's step, accepting it prevents the real cause from being found and fixed. **Accuracy about causes matters more than gallantry.**

---

**S33. Substandard work that nobody noticed. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Leave it | ❌ |
| **(b) Raise it, explain what you would do differently, offer to improve it** | **✅** |
| (c) Improve it quietly and resubmit | ⚠️ Better than nothing, but the reviewer's record is now inconsistent |
| (d) Wait for your next review | ❌ Far too late |

**Answer: (b)**

> **This is a pure integrity scenario disguised as a quality one.** Nobody noticed, and nobody would have. Raising it anyway is exactly the behaviour SJTs are designed to detect — and it is genuinely what strong professionals do.

---

### GROUP F — COMMUNICATION: Solutions 34–37

---

**S34. Colleague has not answered two emails. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Escalate to their manager | ❌ Disproportionate after two emails |
| **(b) Speak to them directly, in person or by phone** | **✅** |
| (c) A third email marked urgent | ❌ Same channel that has already failed twice |
| (d) Proceed without the information | ❌ |

**Answer: (b)**

> **When a channel has failed twice, change the channel.** A thirty-second conversation typically resolves what three emails cannot, and it is far more likely that the colleague is overloaded than that they are ignoring you.

---

**S35. Manager on leave; urgent client question. → (c)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Wait for their return | ❌ The client is waiting |
| (b) Contact the manager on leave | ⚠️ Sometimes right for a genuine emergency, but not the first move |
| **(c) Approach the next appropriate person on the engagement** | **✅** |
| (d) Answer as best you can | ❌ Risks an incorrect client position |

**Answer: (c)**

> **Engagement teams have depth for exactly this reason.** Going to the next appropriate person is the designed path. Option (b) is not wrong in a true emergency, but reaching for it first suggests you have not looked at who else is available.

---

**S36. Manager's instruction rests on a factual misunderstanding. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Follow it as given | ❌ Produces work built on a wrong premise |
| **(b) Check the facts, then raise the specific point with evidence** | **✅** |
| (c) Follow it but note your disagreement | ❌ Documents rather than solves |
| (d) Ask a colleague whether to speak up | ⚠️ Delays without adding information |

**Answer: (b)**

> **"Check the facts first" is what makes this safe to raise.** You might be the one who has misunderstood. Verifying before speaking means that when you do raise it, you bring evidence rather than an impression — which is both more useful and far easier for the manager to receive.

---

**S37. Asked to present with limited experience. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Decline | ❌ Refuses development |
| **(b) Accept, prepare thoroughly, ask for a review and feedback beforehand** | **✅** |
| (c) Accept and prepare alone | ⚠️ Willing but forgoes available support |
| (d) Have a colleague present for you | ❌ |

**Answer: (b)**

> **Asking for a pre-review is not an admission of weakness** — it is what an experienced professional would also do before a client presentation. Option (b) accepts the stretch and manages the risk, which is the combination the scenario is testing for.

---

### GROUP G — INITIATIVE: Solutions 38–40

---

**S38. Automation idea that would save hours. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Implement it immediately, unannounced | ❌ Changes a process without oversight or validation |
| **(b) Explain the idea, including how you would test it, and ask whether to proceed** | **✅** |
| (c) Mention it casually and wait | ⚠️ Under-sells a genuinely valuable idea |
| (d) Keep it to yourself | ❌ |

**Answer: (b)**

> **The clause "including how you would test that it produces the same results" is what makes (b) strong.** Anyone can propose an efficiency; proposing it *with a validation plan* shows you understand that changing a process on a client engagement carries risk as well as benefit.

---

**S39. Inefficient firm-wide process outside your engagement. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Ignore it | ❌ |
| **(b) Raise it constructively with your manager, with a specific suggestion** | **✅** |
| (c) Approach the unknown process owner directly | ⚠️ Well-intentioned but skips your own line |
| (d) Complain to peers | ❌ Generates dissatisfaction without any prospect of change |

**Answer: (b)**

> **"With a specific suggestion" is the operative phrase.** Observing that something is inefficient is a complaint; observing it and proposing an alternative is a contribution. Your manager also knows the right route to the process owner, which you do not.

---

**S40. You are most junior on a call; the team has misread a client requirement. → (b)**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) Stay quiet | ❌ A misunderstanding propagates into the work |
| **(b) Raise it at the appropriate moment, factually and briefly** | **✅** |
| (c) Message a colleague to raise it | ⚠️ Slower and less reliable; the point may be garbled |
| (d) Raise it after the call | ⚠️ Better than silence, but the client has now left with a wrong shared understanding |

**Answer: (b)**

> **Seniority does not determine whether a factual correction is appropriate — only *how* it is delivered.** "At the appropriate moment, factually and briefly" is the whole answer: wait for a natural pause, state the point without drama, and let the team take it from there.
>
> **This scenario tests "the courage to lead" directly.** Staying quiet because you are junior is the failure mode being examined.

## 8. Quick Revision Sheet

> **One page. Read the night before the test — this section is worth more marks than any single quant topic.**

### ⭐ The persona

```
Answer as a CAPABLE FIRST-YEAR ASSOCIATE
    — not a hero, not a passive rule-follower
```

### ⭐ The four-step ladder

```
1.  GATHER INFORMATION   —  understand the facts first
2.  CONSULT              —  usually your immediate manager
3.  ACT                  —  within your authority
4.  ESCALATE             —  transparently, and only after 1–3

The best option sits at the CORRECT RUNG for the scenario as described.
```

### Always ✅ / Never ❌

```
✅ verify before raising a concern
✅ raise integrity issues, however inconvenient
✅ communicate early when something will slip
✅ own mistakes promptly, with an impact assessment and a proposal
✅ help colleagues without abandoning your own commitments
✅ decline improper requests AND offer what help you can
✅ act within your authority; escalate one rung at a time

❌ act unilaterally on incomplete information
❌ conceal, quietly correct, or "wait and see"
❌ go over your manager's head as a FIRST step
❌ contact the client directly without authorisation
❌ blame a colleague before the facts are clear
❌ do nothing because you are junior
❌ heroic all-nighters that hide a resourcing problem
```

### The elimination markers

```
Remove any option containing:

    "without telling anyone"      "quietly"        "ignore it"
    "wait until someone asks"     "assume someone else will notice"
    "contact the client directly" (unless authorised)
    "complain to the partner about your manager" (as a first step)
    "refuse — not my job"

This usually removes TWO of four options in ten seconds.
```

### The two-survivors test

```
□  Which GATHERS INFORMATION first?
□  Which involves the RIGHT PERSON?
□  Which is PROPORTIONATE?
□  Which KEEPS THE WORK MOVING?
□  Which would you be comfortable explaining to a partner?   ← most reliable
```

### The "and" pattern

```
The best answer usually does TWO things:
    addresses the immediate problem  AND  communicates

✅ "Complete what you can  AND  flag the gap to the manager"
✅ "Decline the request    AND  offer to help another way"
✅ "Correct the error      AND  inform the reviewer"
```

### Integrity overrides everything

```
Accuracy · misstatement · confidentiality · independence · falsification

→  ALWAYS raise it
→  PRIVATELY, FACTUALLY, with the RIGHT PERSON
→  Verify first if you can
→  Escalate ONE RUNG if unresolved

There is no SJT scenario where letting a known misstatement stand
is the right answer.
```

### Prioritisation order

```
1.  Client-facing, hard external deadline
2.  Work that BLOCKS a colleague
3.  Internal deadline with flexibility
4.  Self-directed / developmental work

AND ALWAYS: flag a slip EARLY.
A deadline missed with three days' warning is a management problem.
The same deadline missed silently is a trust problem.
```

### Beware the plausible extremes

```
Total self-sacrifice   →  hides a resourcing problem
Total rule-following   →  abdicates judgement
Total independence     →  removes oversight
Total deference        →  abdicates integrity

The right answer lies BETWEEN these poles.
```

### Timing

```
0:00–0:15   Read; identify the competency
0:15–0:30   Eliminate two poor options
0:30–0:50   Apply the two-survivors test
0:50–1:00   Select and move on

DO NOT AGONISE. Over-thinking pushes you toward unusual
responses, which score worse than sensible ones.
```

## 9. Common Mistakes

| # | Mistake | Why it costs marks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Not preparing at all** | 20 of ~56 marks in Pattern A — the largest single block |
| 2 | **Answering as your ideal self** | The scale rewards the *effective* response, not the most virtuous-sounding |
| 3 | **Choosing the heroic option** | All-nighters and taking over others' work hide real problems |
| 4 | **Escalating too far, too fast** | Going to the partner first is disproportionate in most scenarios |
| 5 | **Escalating too little** | Deference on an integrity issue is a serious failure |
| 6 | **Doing nothing because you are junior** | Seniority governs *how* you raise something, not *whether* |
| 7 | **Contacting the client directly** | Correct instinct, wrong channel — this is the most common near-miss |
| 8 | **Concealing to protect a colleague** | Kindness that becomes complicity |
| 9 | **Fixing without telling** | Removes oversight and leaves the record inconsistent |
| 10 | **Accepting blame you may not deserve** | Feels honourable; prevents the real cause being found |
| 11 | **Answering inconsistently across scenarios** | Consistency is itself scored |
| 12 | **Over-thinking** | Drives you toward unusual answers; the sensible one is usually right |
| 13 | **Raising an issue publicly** | Right substance, disproportionate manner |
| 14 | **Refusing without offering an alternative** | "No" alone scores lower than "no, but here is what I can do" |
