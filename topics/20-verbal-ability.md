## 1. Why This Topic Matters for EY

**Weightage:** roughly **50–60% of the verbal section** once Reading Comprehension is set aside. In a 12-question verbal block, expect 5–7 questions from this topic.

**Why EY weights verbal ability seriously:** the firm's core deliverable is *written* — audit reports, advisory decks, client memoranda. A candidate who writes "the number of errors are increasing" in a client document creates a real problem. The verbal section is a proxy for professional writing competence, and EY treats it accordingly.

**The good news:** unlike quantitative topics, verbal ability rewards **pattern recognition over calculation**. The same fifteen grammar rules generate the overwhelming majority of error-spotting and sentence-correction questions.

**Question styles reported:**

| Style | Share of the verbal block |
|---|---|
| **Sentence correction / error spotting** | 12–15% |
| **Fill in the blanks** (grammar + vocabulary) | 12–15% |
| **Synonyms & antonyms** | 12–15% |
| **Para jumbles** | 8–10% |
| **One-word substitution** | 5–7% |
| **Idioms & phrases** | 4–6% |

## 2. Core Concepts — PART A: GRAMMAR

### 2.1 ⭐ Subject–verb agreement — the highest-yield rule set

More error-spotting questions test this than any other single area.

| Rule | Correct form |
|---|---|
| **"The number of…"** takes a **singular** verb | The number of students **is** increasing |
| **"A number of…"** takes a **plural** verb | A number of students **are** absent |
| **Each / Every / Either / Neither** are **singular** | Each of the employees **has** received a bonus |
| **"Along with / as well as / together with"** do not change the subject | The manager, along with his team, **was** present |
| **Collective nouns** (committee, team, jury) are usually **singular** | The committee **has** decided |
| **"One of the…"** takes a **plural** noun and a **singular** verb | One of my friend**s** **is** a doctor |
| **Two subjects joined by "and"** take a **plural** verb | Ravi and Priya **are** here |
| **"Either…or / Neither…nor"** — the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject | Neither the manager nor the staff **were** informed |

> **The "along with" trap deserves special attention.** Phrases like *along with*, *as well as*, *together with*, *in addition to* and *besides* are **parenthetical** — they do not make a singular subject plural.
>
> ✗ The manager along with his team members **were** present
> ✓ The manager along with his team members **was** present

### 2.2 Tense rules

**"Since" vs "For":**

| Word | Used with | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **since** | A **point** in time | since 2019 · since Monday · since last year |
| **for** | A **duration** | for five years · for two hours · for a decade |

> ✗ She has been working here **since** five years
> ✓ She has been working here **for** five years

**Stative verbs are not used in continuous tenses:**

$$\text{know, believe, understand, love, hate, own, belong, resemble, seem}$$

> ✗ I have been **knowing** him for years
> ✓ I have **known** him for years

**Reported speech — the backshift rule:**

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| will | **would** |
| can | **could** |
| is / am | **was** |
| has / have | **had** |
| does | **did** |

> ✗ He said that he **will** come tomorrow
> ✓ He said that he **would** come the next day

### 2.3 Comparatives and prepositions

**Latin comparatives take "to", never "than":**

$$\text{senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior, preferable, anterior, posterior}$$

> ✗ He is senior **than** me → ✓ He is senior **to** me
> ✗ This is superior **than** that → ✓ This is superior **to** that

**"Prefer" takes "to", not "than":**

> ✗ I prefer coffee **than** tea → ✓ I prefer coffee **to** tea
> *(But: "I would prefer to walk **rather than** drive" is correct.)*

**Fixed prepositions worth memorising:**

| Verb / adjective | Preposition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| depend | **on** | It depends on the outcome |
| consist | **of** | The team consists of six people |
| comprise | **(nothing)** | The team comprises six people ✓ (*not* "comprises of") |
| accuse | **of** | He was accused of negligence |
| capable | **of** | She is capable of leading |
| responsible | **for** | He is responsible for the report |
| interfere | **with / in** | Do not interfere with the process |
| deprive | **of** | They were deprived of resources |
| famous / known | **for** | He is known for his punctuality |
| married | **to** | She is married to an engineer |

### 2.4 Articles

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| **a** before a consonant *sound* | a university, a European, a one-rupee coin |
| **an** before a vowel *sound* | an hour, an honest man, an MBA |
| **the** for something specific or previously mentioned | the report I sent you |
| No article before an abstract noun in general use | Honesty is the best policy |

> **The rule is about SOUND, not spelling.** "University" begins with a *yoo* sound, so it takes **a**. "Hour" begins with a vowel sound (the *h* is silent), so it takes **an**.

### 2.5 Common structural rules

| Structure | Correct form |
|---|---|
| **Hardly … when** | Hardly had he arrived **when** the meeting began |
| **Scarcely … when** | Scarcely had she left **when** it rained |
| **No sooner … than** | No sooner had he spoken **than** the crowd cheered |
| **Not only … but also** | Not only the report **but also** the annexures were sent |
| **Neither … nor** | Neither the manager **nor** the staff attended |
| **Either … or** | Either you **or** he must attend |
| **Lest … should** | He worked hard **lest** he **should** fail |
| **Negative infinitive** | She asked him **not to** talk (*not* "to not talk") |

> **Inversion after "Hardly", "Scarcely", "No sooner" and "Seldom".** When a sentence begins with these, the auxiliary comes before the subject:
> ✓ **Hardly had he** arrived… (*not* "Hardly he had arrived")

### 2.6 Modifiers

A modifier must sit **next to** the word it modifies.

> ✗ Walking down the street, the building looked impressive.
> *(This says the building was walking.)*
> ✓ Walking down the street, **I** thought the building looked impressive.

> ✗ He **only** eats vegetables on Sundays.
> ✓ He eats **only** vegetables on Sundays. *(or)* He eats vegetables **only** on Sundays.

## 3. Core Concepts — PART B: VOCABULARY

### 3.1 Attacking an unknown word

```
STEP 1:  Do you know the word?           →  answer directly
STEP 2:  Do you recognise a ROOT?        →  infer the meaning
STEP 3:  Is there a PREFIX?              →  positive or negative direction
STEP 4:  Eliminate options that clearly
         point the WRONG WAY
STEP 5:  Guess among what remains — there is no negative marking
```

### 3.2 Prefixes that set direction

**Negative / reversing:**

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **a-, an-** | without | atypical, anonymous |
| **anti-** | against | antidote, antithesis |
| **de-** | reverse, remove | devalue, deregulate |
| **dis-** | not, apart | disagree, dissuade |
| **il-, im-, in-, ir-** | not | illegible, impartial, inept, irregular |
| **mal-** | bad | malfunction, malevolent |
| **mis-** | wrongly | misinterpret, misconstrue |
| **non-** | not | nonchalant, nonentity |
| **un-** | not | unwarranted, unequivocal |

**Positive / intensifying:**

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **ben-, bene-** | good | benevolent, beneficial |
| **eu-** | good, well | euphemism, eulogy |
| **pro-** | forward, in favour | proponent, progressive |
| **super-, sur-** | above, beyond | superfluous, surpass |

### 3.3 Roots worth knowing

| Root | Meaning | Words |
|---|---|---|
| **-dict-** | say | predict, contradict, verdict |
| **-duc-** | lead | conduct, induce, deduce |
| **-fer-** | carry | transfer, infer, defer |
| **-graph-** | write | biography, graphic |
| **-luc-, -lum-** | light | lucid, elucidate, illuminate |
| **-cred-** | believe | credible, incredulous |
| **-loqu-, -locu-** | speak | eloquent, loquacious, colloquial |
| **-ten-, -tain-** | hold | tenacious, retain, sustain |
| **-vol-** | wish | benevolent, malevolent, volition |
| **-verb-** | word | verbose, verbatim |

> **Two worked inferences:**
> - **LUCID** — root *luc-* = light ⇒ "letting light through" ⇒ **clear** ✓
> - **TENACIOUS** — root *ten-* = hold ⇒ "holding on" ⇒ **persistent** ✓

### 3.4 High-frequency business vocabulary

Words that recur in professional-services assessments:

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **discrepancy** | An inconsistency between figures or accounts |
| **mitigate** | To reduce the severity of something |
| **prudent** | Showing careful, sensible judgement |
| **meticulous** | Extremely careful about detail |
| **candid** | Frank and honest |
| **ambiguous** | Open to more than one interpretation |
| **concise** | Brief but comprehensive |
| **volatile** | Liable to sudden change |
| **obsolete** | No longer in use; outdated |
| **augment** | To increase or add to |
| **alleviate** | To make less severe |
| **frugal** | Sparing with money or resources |
| **reluctant** | Unwilling or hesitant |
| **tenacious** | Persistent; not letting go |
| **amiable** | Friendly and pleasant |
| **abundant** | Present in large quantity |

### 3.5 Frequently confused pairs

| Pair | Distinction |
|---|---|
| **affect / effect** | *affect* = verb (to influence) · *effect* = noun (a result) |
| **principal / principle** | *principal* = chief, or head of a school · *principle* = a rule |
| **stationary / stationery** | *stationary* = not moving · *stationery* = writing materials |
| **complement / compliment** | *complement* = completes · *compliment* = praise |
| **deft / daft** | *deft* = skilful · *daft* = silly |
| **eminent / imminent** | *eminent* = distinguished · *imminent* = about to happen |
| **elicit / illicit** | *elicit* = to draw out · *illicit* = unlawful |
| **adverse / averse** | *adverse* = unfavourable · *averse* = opposed to |
| **discreet / discrete** | *discreet* = tactful · *discrete* = separate |
| **assure / ensure / insure** | *assure* a person · *ensure* an outcome · *insure* property |

## 4. Core Concepts — PART C: PARA JUMBLES

### 4.1 The method

```
STEP 1:  Find the OPENING sentence
             — introduces the topic
             — contains no back-reference (no "it", "this", "however", "such")
             — often the most general statement

STEP 2:  Find MANDATORY PAIRS
             — a pronoun and its antecedent
             — a cause and its effect
             — a claim and its evidence

STEP 3:  Follow the CONNECTORS
             — however / but / yet         →  contrast: needs a preceding claim
             — therefore / thus / hence    →  conclusion: needs preceding reasons
             — also / moreover / further   →  addition: needs a preceding point
             — for example / such as       →  illustration: needs a preceding claim

STEP 4:  Find the CLOSING sentence
             — a conclusion, consequence, or present-day statement

STEP 5:  Check the options — often two are eliminated by the opener alone
```

### 4.2 Opener signals

| Likely opener | Unlikely opener |
|---|---|
| A general statement of the topic | Begins with "It", "This", "These", "Such" |
| Introduces a named subject in full | Begins with "However", "Therefore", "Also" |
| Sets the scene or context | Contains "the above", "as mentioned" |
| Poses the question the passage answers | Refers to something not yet introduced |

### 4.3 Chronology and logic chains

Many jumbles follow a natural sequence:

```
GENERAL  →  SPECIFIC
PROBLEM  →  CAUSE  →  CONSEQUENCE  →  SOLUTION
PAST     →  PRESENT  →  FUTURE
CLAIM    →  EVIDENCE  →  CONCLUSION
```

Look for time markers: *first, initially, later, subsequently, today, now, eventually*.

### 4.4 The elimination shortcut

You rarely need to build the whole sequence. Identify the **first** sentence and the options usually collapse to one or two. If the first sentence is ambiguous, identify the **last**, or find one **certain adjacent pair** and eliminate every option that separates them.

## 5. Shortcuts & Tricks

### 5.1 The subject-hunting technique

For every subject–verb question, **find the true subject and ignore everything between it and the verb**.

```
"The manager, along with his team members, ___ present."
 └── TRUE SUBJECT ──┘ └─── ignore this ───┘

Subject = "The manager" (singular)  →  WAS
```

Physically cross out the intervening phrase on your rough sheet.

### 5.2 The fifteen rules that cover most error-spotting

```
 1.  The number of + singular verb      / A number of + plural verb
 2.  Each, every, either, neither       → singular
 3.  Along with / as well as            → does not change the subject
 4.  Collective nouns                   → singular
 5.  One of the + plural noun + singular verb
 6.  since = point in time              / for = duration
 7.  Stative verbs                      → never continuous
 8.  Reported speech                    → backshift (will → would)
 9.  senior/junior/superior/inferior/prefer  → "to", never "than"
10.  a / an by SOUND, not spelling
11.  Hardly … when · No sooner … than · Scarcely … when
12.  Inversion after Hardly / Scarcely / No sooner / Seldom
13.  "not to" — never "to not"
14.  comprise (no "of") · consist OF
15.  Modifier must sit next to what it modifies
```

### 5.3 Vocabulary — the direction test

Even without knowing a word, its **prefix** usually reveals whether the answer is positive or negative.

```
Word starts with mal-, mis-, dis-, un-, in-, il-, ir-, anti-, non-
    →  the meaning is likely NEGATIVE
    →  in a SYNONYM question, eliminate positive options
    →  in an ANTONYM question, eliminate negative options
```

This halves the option set in seconds.

### 5.4 Para jumbles — the opener filter

```
Cross out any sentence that begins with:
    It · This · These · Such · However · Therefore · Also · Moreover

These almost never open a paragraph — they refer back to something.
What remains is your opener. Check the options and eliminate.
```

### 5.5 Sentence correction — read the option into the sentence

Do not judge options in isolation. **Substitute each one into the full sentence and read it aloud in your head.** Errors that are invisible as fragments become obvious in context.

### 5.6 The "no correction required" option

It is correct roughly **one time in six**. Do not automatically reject it — but do check the sentence against the fifteen rules before selecting it.

### 5.7 Sanity checks

```
✓ Locate the TRUE subject; ignore the intervening phrase
✓ "The number of" is singular; "a number of" is plural
✓ "since" needs a point in time; "for" needs a duration
✓ Latin comparatives take "to", never "than"
✓ "a/an" follows SOUND
✓ Para jumbles: openers never begin with a back-reference
✓ Verbal has no negative marking — never leave a blank
```

## 6. Solved Examples

### Example 1 — Subject–verb agreement

**Q.** Identify the part containing the error.
**(a)** The list of items **(b)** that were mentioned **(c)** in the report are **(d)** now available.

**Step 1 — Find the true subject.**
```
The list  of items that were mentioned in the report   ARE
└─SUBJECT─┘ └────── all modifiers ──────┘              ↑
                                                    the verb
```
The subject is **"The list"** — singular. Everything from "of items" onwards is a modifying phrase.

**Step 2 — Match the verb.**
$$\text{Singular subject} \implies \textbf{is}, \text{ not "are"}$$

**Step 3 — Locate the error.**
The word "are" appears in part **(c)**.

**Corrected sentence:** *The list of items that were mentioned in the report **is** now available.*

**Answer: (c)**

> **Why "were" in part (b) is correct.** That verb belongs to the relative clause "that were mentioned", whose subject is **items** (plural). Two different verbs in the same sentence can legitimately take different numbers.

---

### Example 2 — "Since" vs "for"

**Q.** Identify the error.
**(a)** She has been teaching **(b)** at this institute **(c)** since eight years **(d)** without a break.

**Step 1 — Apply the rule.**
```
since  →  a POINT in time    (since 2016, since Monday, since she graduated)
for    →  a DURATION         (for eight years, for two hours)
```

**Step 2 — Classify "eight years".**
This is a **duration**, not a point in time ⇒ it requires **for**.

**Corrected sentence:** *She has been teaching at this institute **for** eight years without a break.*

**Answer: (c)**

---

### Example 3 — Comparatives taking "to"

**Q.** Choose the correct sentence.
(a) He is senior than me in the department.
(b) He is senior to me in the department.
(c) He is more senior than me in the department.
(d) He is seniorer than me in the department.

**Step 1 — Recall the rule.**
Comparatives of Latin origin take **"to"**, never "than":
$$\text{senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior, preferable}$$

**Step 2 — Evaluate the options.**

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| (a) "senior than" | ✗ Wrong preposition |
| **(b) "senior to"** | **✓ Correct** |
| (c) "more senior than" | ✗ *Senior* is already comparative — "more" is redundant |
| (d) "seniorer" | ✗ Not a word |

**Answer: (b) He is senior to me in the department.**

---

### Example 4 — Reported speech

**Q.** Identify the error.
**(a)** The manager announced **(b)** that the results **(c)** will be declared **(d)** the following week.

**Step 1 — Identify the reporting verb's tense.**
"announced" is **past tense** ⇒ the reported clause must **backshift**.

**Step 2 — Apply the backshift.**

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| will | **would** |
| can | could |
| is | was |
| has | had |

**Step 3 — Locate the error.**
"will be declared" should be "**would** be declared" — part **(c)**.

**Corrected sentence:** *The manager announced that the results **would** be declared the following week.*

**Answer: (c)**

> **Note the consistency of "the following week".** In reported speech, *tomorrow* becomes *the next day*, *yesterday* becomes *the previous day*, and *next week* becomes *the following week*. The sentence gets this right; only the modal is wrong.

### Example 5 — Vocabulary via roots

**Q.** Choose the word most nearly similar in meaning to **LUCID**.
(a) Confusing  (b) Clear  (c) Shadowy  (d) Complicated

**Step 1 — Identify the root.**
$$\textbf{LUC-} = \text{light} \quad (\text{as in \emph{elucidate}, \emph{translucent}, \emph{illuminate}})$$

**Step 2 — Infer the direction.**
A root meaning "light" points to something **transparent, easily seen through** ⇒ positive, clarity-related.

**Step 3 — Eliminate.**
Options (a), (c) and (d) all point toward *obscurity* — the opposite direction.

**Answer: (b) Clear**

> **This is the root method in action.** Even without knowing *lucid*, the *luc-* root plus the direction test isolates the answer.

---

### Example 6 — Fill in the blank (confusable pair)

**Q.** The auditor identified several ______ between the invoices and the ledger entries.
(a) discrepancies  (b) agreements  (c) compliments  (d) endorsements

**Step 1 — Read the context.**
An auditor comparing invoices with ledger entries is looking for **mismatches**. The word "between" and the audit setting both point to a comparison finding a *problem*.

**Step 2 — Evaluate the options.**

| Option | Meaning | Fits? |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) discrepancies** | Inconsistencies between figures | **✓** |
| (b) agreements | Things matching | ✗ Would not be "identified" as noteworthy |
| (c) compliments | Expressions of praise | ✗ Wrong register entirely |
| (d) endorsements | Approvals | ✗ Not something found between records |

**Answer: (a) discrepancies**

> **Watch for the compliment/complement trap.** *Compliment* is praise; *complement* is something that completes. Neither fits here, but the two are constantly offered together.

### Example 7 — Para jumble

**Q.** Arrange the following into a coherent paragraph.

**P.** However, it requires careful planning and the right infrastructure.
**Q.** Remote work offers considerable flexibility to employees.
**R.** Companies must therefore invest in secure collaboration tools.
**S.** It also reduces commuting time and costs.

Options: (a) QSPR  (b) QPSR  (c) SQPR  (d) PQSR

**Step 1 — Find the opener.**
```
P — begins with "However"      →  needs a preceding claim to contrast  ✗
Q — introduces the topic fully →  "Remote work offers…"                ✓
R — begins with "therefore"    →  needs preceding reasoning             ✗
S — begins with "It also"      →  "It" refers back; "also" adds          ✗
```
$$\implies \textbf{Q is the opener}$$

Eliminate option (c) and (d) immediately.

**Step 2 — Find what follows Q.**
S says "**It also** reduces…" — the word *also* adds a second benefit to the first one in Q. This is a mandatory pair.
$$\implies \textbf{Q} \to \textbf{S}$$

Option (b) has Q → P, so eliminate it. Only **(a) QSPR** remains.

**Step 3 — Verify the rest.**
- **P** begins with "However" — it turns from the two benefits to a caveat ✓
- **R** begins with "therefore" — it draws the consequence of P's caveat ✓

**The assembled paragraph:**
> *Remote work offers considerable flexibility to employees. It also reduces commuting time and costs. However, it requires careful planning and the right infrastructure. Companies must therefore invest in secure collaboration tools.*

**Answer: (a) QSPR**

### Example 8 — Modifier placement

**Q.** Choose the correct sentence.
(a) Having reviewed the accounts, the discrepancy was obvious to the auditor.
(b) Having reviewed the accounts, the auditor found the discrepancy obvious.
(c) Having reviewed the accounts, it was obvious the discrepancy.
(d) The discrepancy, having reviewed the accounts, was obvious.

**Step 1 — Identify the modifier.**
"Having reviewed the accounts" is a participial phrase. It must modify **whoever did the reviewing**.

**Step 2 — Test each option.**

| Option | What the phrase modifies | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | "the discrepancy" | ✗ A discrepancy cannot review accounts |
| **(b)** | **"the auditor"** | **✓ The auditor did the reviewing** |
| (c) | "it" | ✗ Dangling; also ungrammatical word order |
| (d) | "the discrepancy" | ✗ Same dangling error, worse placement |

**Answer: (b) Having reviewed the accounts, the auditor found the discrepancy obvious.**

> **The dangling-modifier test:** ask *"who or what performed the action in the opening phrase?"* That noun must appear **immediately after the comma**.

### 🟡 ERROR SPOTTING (Questions 25–32)

### 🟡 ERROR SPOTTING — Solutions 25–32

---

**Q25. "The number of students in the class are increasing." → (c)**

**Rule applied:** *"The number of…"* takes a **singular** verb.

```
The number of  →  SINGULAR   ("The number of students IS increasing")
A number of    →  PLURAL     ("A number of students ARE absent")
```

The error is **"are"** in part (c).

**Corrected:** *The number of students in the class **is** increasing every year.*

**Answer: (c)**

---

**Q26. "He is one of the best player in the team." → (b)**

**Rule applied:** *"one of the…"* is followed by a **plural noun**.

$$\text{one of the best player\textbf{s}}$$

The logic: he is one member of a group of best players, so the group must be plural.

**Corrected:** *He is one of the best **players** in the team this season.*

**Answer: (b)**

---

**Q27. "Neither of the two candidates were selected." → (b)**

**Rule applied:** *neither*, *either*, *each* and *every* take **singular** verbs.

$$\text{Neither of the two candidates } \textbf{was} \text{ selected}$$

> **Why the plural feels natural:** "candidates" sits right before the verb. But the true subject is **"Neither"**, which is singular. This is the same subject-hunting discipline as in Q25.

**Corrected:** *Neither of the two candidates **was** selected for the position.*

**Answer: (b)**

---

**Q28. "She has been working in this company since five years." → (c)**

**Rule applied:**
```
since  →  a POINT in time     (since 2019, since Monday, since she joined)
for    →  a DURATION          (for five years, for two hours)
```

"Five years" is a duration ⇒ requires **for**.

**Corrected:** *She has been working in this company **for** five years without a break.*

**Answer: (c)**

---

**Q29. "The manager along with his team members were present." → (c)**

**Rule applied:** *along with*, *as well as*, *together with* and *in addition to* are **parenthetical** — they do not change the number of the subject.

```
The manager  along with his team members   WERE
└─SUBJECT──┘ └──── ignore this phrase ───┘  ↑
   singular                              should be WAS
```

**Corrected:** *The manager along with his team members **was** present at the meeting.*

> **Contrast with "and":** *"The manager **and** his team members **were** present"* is correct — *and* genuinely creates a compound plural subject.

**Answer: (c)**

---

**Q30. "I have been knowing him for many years." → (b)**

**Rule applied:** **stative verbs** are not used in continuous tenses.

$$\text{know, believe, understand, love, hate, own, belong, resemble, seem}$$

These describe states, not ongoing actions, so they take simple rather than continuous forms.

**Corrected:** *I have **known** him for many years now.*

**Answer: (b)**

---

**Q31. "Each of the employees have received a bonus." → (b)**

**Rule applied:** *each* is **singular**, regardless of the plural noun that follows.

$$\text{Each of the employees } \textbf{has} \text{ received}$$

**Corrected:** *Each of the employees **has** received a bonus this quarter.*

**Answer: (b)**

---

**Q32. "He said that he will come to the office the next day." → (b)**

**Rule applied:** in reported speech following a **past-tense** reporting verb, modals **backshift**.

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| will | **would** |
| can | could |
| shall | should |
| may | might |

**Corrected:** *He said that he **would** come to the office the next day.*

> **Note that "the next day" is already correct** — the time expression has been properly backshifted from "tomorrow". Only the modal was left unchanged.

**Answer: (b)**

---

**Q33. "One of my friend is a doctor." → (a) One of my friends is**

**Two rules interact here:**
1. *"one of the…"* takes a **plural noun** ⇒ friend**s**
2. *"one"* is the subject ⇒ **singular verb** ⇒ **is**

| Option | Noun | Verb | Correct? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) One of my friends is** | plural ✓ | singular ✓ | **✓** |
| (b) One of my friend are | singular ✗ | plural ✗ | ✗ |
| (c) One of my friends are | plural ✓ | plural ✗ | ✗ |
| (d) No correction | singular ✗ | — | ✗ |

**Answer: (a) One of my friends is**

**Q34. "He is senior than me." → (a) Senior to me**

**Rule applied:** Latin comparatives take **"to"**, never "than":
$$\text{senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior, preferable}$$

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **(a) Senior to me** | **✓ Correct** |
| (b) More senior than me | ✗ *Senior* is already comparative; "more" is redundant, and "than" is wrong |
| (c) Senior than I | ✗ Still uses "than" |
| (d) No correction | ✗ |

**Answer: (a) Senior to me**

---

**Q35. "The committee have decided…" → (a) Has decided**

**Rule applied:** collective nouns (committee, team, jury, government, board) take a **singular** verb when acting as a single unit.

**Corrected:** *The committee **has decided** to postpone the meeting.*

> **The exception worth knowing:** when the members are acting *individually and in disagreement*, the plural is sometimes used — "The jury were divided in their opinions." But in placement tests, the singular is the expected answer.

**Answer: (a) Has decided**

---

**Q36. "I prefer coffee than tea." → (a) Prefer coffee to tea**

**Rule applied:** *prefer* takes **"to"**, not "than".

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **(a) Prefer coffee to tea** | **✓ Correct** |
| (b) Prefer coffee than to tea | ✗ Ungrammatical |
| (c) Prefer coffee more than tea | ✗ "More" is redundant with *prefer* |
| (d) No correction | ✗ |

> **The one case where "rather than" is correct:** with infinitives — *"I would prefer to walk **rather than** drive."* But with nouns, it is always *prefer X **to** Y*.

**Answer: (a) Prefer coffee to tea**

**Q37. "Hardly had he arrived when the meeting started." → (a) No correction required**

**Two rules are already satisfied:**

1. **Inversion.** After an opening *Hardly*, the auxiliary precedes the subject:
   $$\text{Hardly } \textbf{had he} \text{ arrived} \quad ✓ \quad (\text{not "Hardly he had arrived"})$$

2. **Correlative pairing.**
   ```
   Hardly    … when    ✓
   Scarcely  … when    ✓
   No sooner … than    ✓
   ```

The sentence uses *Hardly … when* correctly with proper inversion.

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **(a) No correction required** | **✓** |
| (b) Hardly he had arrived when | ✗ No inversion |
| (c) Hardly had he arrived than | ✗ "than" pairs with *no sooner*, not *hardly* |
| (d) Hardly had he arrived then | ✗ "then" is not a correlative |

**Answer: (a) No correction required**

> **"No correction required" is right about one time in six.** Check the sentence against the rules rather than assuming an error must exist.

**Rule applied:** in the negative infinitive, **"not" precedes "to"**.

$$\textbf{not to talk} \quad ✓ \qquad \text{to not talk} \quad ✗$$

**Corrected:** *The teacher asked the students **not to talk** during the examination.*

**Answer: (a) Not to talk**

**Q39. AI transforming business → (a) QSRP**

```
P — "It has transformed…"        →  "It" refers back                    ✗
Q — "Artificial intelligence is  →  names the subject in full           ✓
     no longer a futuristic
     concept."
R — "From customer service to    →  an incomplete phrase; needs a       ✗
     supply chains,"                 main clause to follow
S — "It is embedded…"            →  "It" refers back                    ✗
```
$$\implies \textbf{Q opens}$$

Options (c) and (d) are eliminated.

**Step 2 — Find the mandatory pair.**
R ends with a comma and is grammatically incomplete: *"From customer service to supply chains,"* It **must** be followed by a main clause. P (*"it has transformed how businesses operate"*) completes it naturally.
$$\implies \textbf{R} \to \textbf{P}$$

Option (b) has R → S, so it is eliminated. Only **(a) QSRP** remains.

**Step 3 — Verify.**
> *Artificial intelligence is no longer a futuristic concept. It is embedded in everyday business processes. From customer service to supply chains, it has transformed how businesses operate.*

The sequence runs: claim → elaboration → scope-and-impact ✓

**Q40. Remote work → (a) QSPR**

**Step 1 — Opener.**
```
P — "However…"      →  needs a preceding claim to contrast  ✗
Q — "Remote work    →  introduces the topic                 ✓
     offers…"
R — "Companies must →  a consequence; needs preceding reason ✗
     therefore…"
S — "It also…"      →  back-reference plus addition          ✗
```
$$\implies \textbf{Q opens}$$

**Step 2 — Mandatory pair.**
S begins *"It **also** reduces…"* — *also* adds a second benefit to the one stated in Q.
$$\implies \textbf{Q} \to \textbf{S}$$

**Step 3 — The remaining sequence.**
- P (*"However…"*) turns from benefits to a caveat
- R (*"…must therefore invest…"*) draws the consequence of that caveat

$$\implies \textbf{Q} \to \textbf{S} \to \textbf{P} \to \textbf{R}$$

**Verify:**
> *Remote work offers flexibility to employees. It also reduces commuting costs. However, it requires careful planning. Companies must invest in the right tools.*

**Answer: (a) QSPR**

**Q41. Exercise benefits → (a) PQSR**

**Step 1 — Opener.**
```
P — "Regular exercise improves…"   →  a direct topic statement   ✓
Q — "It also enhances…"            →  back-reference + addition  ✗
R — "Doctors therefore…"           →  conclusion                 ✗
S — "Numerous studies confirm      →  "these benefits" refers    ✗
     these benefits."                  back
```
$$\implies \textbf{P opens}$$

**Step 2 — Build the logical chain.**
```
P  →  first benefit (physical)
Q  →  second benefit ("also")
S  →  evidence for BOTH ("these benefits" — plural, so it must follow both P and Q)
R  →  conclusion ("therefore")
```

$$\implies \textbf{P} \to \textbf{Q} \to \textbf{S} \to \textbf{R}$$

**Verify:**
> *Regular exercise improves cardiovascular health. It also enhances mental well-being. Numerous studies confirm these benefits. Doctors therefore recommend at least thirty minutes daily.*

The structure is claim → claim → evidence → recommendation ✓

> **The plural "these benefits" in S is the decisive clue.** It requires **both** P and Q to precede it, which fixes S in third position.

**Answer: (a) PQSR**

**Q42. Product launch → (a) QRPS**

```

Q — "The company launched…"   →  introduces the event                  ✓
R — "Sales grew by 45%…"      →  a specific figure; needs context      ✗
S — "Management has now…"     →  "now" and "second" signal a later step ✗
```
$$\implies \textbf{Q opens}$$

```
Q  →  the event (product launched)
R  →  the data (sales grew 45%)
P  →  the evaluation (results exceeded expectations)
S  →  the consequence (second product line approved)
```

$$\implies \textbf{Q} \to \textbf{R} \to \textbf{P} \to \textbf{S}$$

**Verify:**
> *The company launched a new product last quarter. Sales grew by 45% within three months. The results exceeded all expectations. Management has now approved a second product line.*

Event → data → evaluation → consequence ✓

> **Why R precedes P.** The specific figure (45%) is the *evidence*; "exceeded all expectations" is the *judgement* drawn from it. Evidence comes before evaluation.

**Answer: (a) QRPS**

**Q43. Reporting standards → (a) RQPS**

```

Q — "Different countries use…"  →  a fact, but see step 2          ~
R — "Global companies face a    →  states the problem in general    ✓
     persistent reporting
     challenge."

                                    stated first
```
$$\implies \textbf{R opens}$$ — it announces the topic in the most general terms.

**Step 2 — Build the chain.**
```
R  →  the problem (a reporting challenge exists)
Q  →  the cause (different standards)
P  →  the consequence ("This makes it difficult…")
S  →  the response (harmonisation efforts)
```

$$\implies \textbf{R} \to \textbf{Q} \to \textbf{P} \to \textbf{S}$$

**Verify:**
> *Global companies face a persistent reporting challenge. Different countries use different accounting standards. This makes it difficult to compare performance across regions. Efforts to harmonise standards are ongoing.*

Problem → cause → consequence → response ✓

> **"This" in P is the pivotal clue.** It must refer to the *immediately preceding* idea — the different standards in Q. That fixes Q → P as a mandatory pair.

**Answer: (a) RQPS**

---

**Q44. History of a method → (a) QSPR**

**Step 1 — Opener.**
```
P — "It was later adopted…"  →  "later" than what? back-reference   ✗
Q — "The method was first     →  "first proposed" — the origin       ✓
     proposed in a 1962
     paper."
R — "Today it is…"            →  the present-day endpoint            ✗
S — "Initially, it attracted  →  "Initially" follows the origin      ✗
     little attention."
```
$$\implies \textbf{Q opens}$$

**Step 2 — Follow the chronology.**
The time markers lay out the sequence explicitly:

| Sentence | Time marker | Position |
|---|---|---|
| Q | "first proposed", 1962 | **1st** |
| S | "Initially" | **2nd** |
| P | "later" | **3rd** |
| R | "Today" | **4th** |

$$\implies \textbf{Q} \to \textbf{S} \to \textbf{P} \to \textbf{R}$$

**Verify:**
> *The method was first proposed in a 1962 paper. Initially, it attracted little attention. It was later adopted by universities worldwide. Today it is a standard part of the curriculum.*

**Answer: (a) QSPR**

> **Chronological jumbles are the easiest type.** Scan for time markers — *first, initially, later, subsequently, today, now* — and order them directly.

### 🔴 ONE-WORD SUBSTITUTION — Solutions 45–48

---

**Q45. A person who speaks many languages → (b) Polyglot**

**Root method:** *poly-* = many · *-glot* = tongue, language

| Option | Meaning |
|---|---|
| (a) Linguist | A student of *language as a subject* — not necessarily a speaker of many |
| **(b) Polyglot** | **A speaker of many languages** ✓ |
| (c) Orator | A skilled public speaker |
| (d) Bilingual | A speaker of exactly **two** languages |

> **Note the precision required.** *Bilingual* is too narrow (two languages) and *linguist* denotes an academic discipline. Only *polyglot* means "many languages spoken".

**Answer: (b) Polyglot**

---

**Q46. One who can neither read nor write → (b) Illiterate**

| Option | Meaning |
|---|---|
| (a) Ignorant | Lacking knowledge generally — broader and different |
| **(b) Illiterate** | **Unable to read or write** ✓ |
| (c) Illegible | Describes *handwriting* that cannot be read |
| (d) Illegal | Contrary to law |

> **Illiterate / illegible** is a frequent confusion. *Illiterate* describes a **person**; *illegible* describes **writing**.

**Answer: (b) Illiterate**

---

**Q47. Government by the people → (b) Democracy**

**Root method:** *demos* = people · *-cracy* = rule

| Term | Rule by |
|---|---|
| Monarchy | A single hereditary ruler |
| **Democracy** | **The people** ✓ |
| Aristocracy | A privileged/noble class |
| Oligarchy | A small group |

**Answer: (b) Democracy**

---

**Q48. A statement open to more than one interpretation → (a) Ambiguous**

| Option | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **(a) Ambiguous** | **Open to more than one interpretation** ✓ |
| (b) Obvious | Clear and evident — the opposite |
| (c) Explicit | Stated plainly and exactly — the opposite |
| (d) Unequivocal | Admitting no doubt — the opposite |

> **Three of the four options are near-antonyms.** Where a definition emphasises *uncertainty*, expect the distractors to emphasise *clarity*.

**Answer: (a) Ambiguous**

### 🔴 IDIOMS — Solutions 49–50

---

**Q49. To bite the bullet → (b) To endure a painful situation with courage**

**Meaning:** to accept and face something unpleasant or difficult that cannot be avoided.

**Origin:** wounded soldiers were reportedly given a bullet to bite on during surgery performed without anaesthetic.

**Usage:** *"With the deadline unchanged, the team bit the bullet and worked through the weekend."*

**Answer: (b) To endure a painful situation with courage**

---

**Q50. To let the cat out of the bag → (b) To reveal a secret**

**Meaning:** to disclose something that was meant to be kept confidential, often accidentally.

**Origin:** commonly traced to a market trick in which a cat was substituted for a piglet in a sack; opening the bag exposed the deception.

**Usage:** *"He let the cat out of the bag about the merger before the announcement."*

**Answer: (b) To reveal a secret**

> **Idioms are pure memory work.** They cannot be derived from their component words — "bite the bullet" has nothing to do with either biting or bullets in ordinary use. Learn the twenty or so that recur most often (see the revision sheet).

## 9. Quick Revision Sheet

> **One page. Read 48 hours after study, and again the night before the test.**

### ⭐ The fifteen grammar rules

```
 1.  "The number of" + SINGULAR verb   |  "A number of" + PLURAL verb
 2.  Each · Every · Either · Neither   →  SINGULAR
 3.  along with / as well as / together with  →  does NOT change the subject
 4.  Collective nouns (committee, team, jury) →  SINGULAR
 5.  "One of the" + PLURAL noun + SINGULAR verb
 6.  since = POINT in time      |  for = DURATION
 7.  Stative verbs (know, believe, own, love) →  NEVER continuous
 8.  Reported speech: will→would, can→could, is→was, has→had
 9.  senior / junior / superior / inferior / prefer  →  "TO", never "than"
10.  a / an by SOUND, not spelling  (a university · an hour · an MBA)
11.  Hardly…when · Scarcely…when · No sooner…than
12.  Inversion after Hardly / Scarcely / No sooner / Seldom
13.  "not to talk" ✓   not "to not talk" ✗
14.  comprise (NO "of")  |  consist OF
15.  A modifier must sit next to what it modifies
```

**The subject-hunting technique**

```
"The manager, along with his team members, ___ present."
 └── TRUE SUBJECT ──┘  └──── cross this out ────┘
                                                  → WAS
```

### Fixed prepositions

```
depend ON · consist OF · comprise (nothing) · accuse OF · capable OF
responsible FOR · deprive OF · known FOR · married TO · interfere WITH
```

### Confusable pairs

```
affect (v) / effect (n)          principal (chief) / principle (rule)
stationary (still) / stationery (paper)
complement (completes) / compliment (praise)
deft (skilful) / daft (silly)    eminent (famous) / imminent (about to happen)
elicit (draw out) / illicit (unlawful)
adverse (unfavourable) / averse (opposed)
discreet (tactful) / discrete (separate)
achieved / archived              assure (person) / ensure (outcome) / insure (property)
```

### Vocabulary — the direction test

```
NEGATIVE PREFIXES:  a- an- anti- de- dis- il- im- in- ir- mal- mis- non- un-
POSITIVE PREFIXES:  bene- eu- pro- super- sur-

ROOTS:  dict (say) · duc (lead) · fer (carry) · graph (write)
        luc/lum (light) · cred (believe) · loqu (speak)
        ten/tain (hold) · vol (wish) · verb (word)

LUCID → luc = light → CLEAR
TENACIOUS → ten = hold → PERSISTENT
BENEVOLENT vs MALEVOLENT → bene/mal + vol = wishing good/harm
VERBOSE → verb = word → too many words
```

**In ANTONYM questions, three options are usually SYNONYMS of the given word.**
Find the odd one out.

### PARA JUMBLES

```
STEP 1 — OPENER: cross out any sentence beginning with
             It · This · These · Such · However · Therefore · Also · Moreover
         What remains is the opener. Eliminate options.

STEP 2 — MANDATORY PAIRS: pronoun↔antecedent · cause↔effect · claim↔evidence

STEP 3 — CONNECTORS
             however / but      →  needs a preceding claim
             therefore / thus   →  needs preceding reasoning
             also / moreover    →  needs a preceding point
             for example        →  needs a preceding claim

STEP 4 — SEQUENCES
             GENERAL → SPECIFIC
             PROBLEM → CAUSE → CONSEQUENCE → SOLUTION
             PAST → PRESENT → FUTURE  (first, initially, later, today)
             CLAIM → EVIDENCE → CONCLUSION
```

### Test-day tactics

```
✓ Read each correction option INTO the sentence, don't judge fragments
✓ "No correction required" is right about 1 time in 6 — verify, don't assume
✓ In vocabulary, use the prefix to fix DIRECTION, then eliminate
✓ In jumbles, find the opener FIRST — it usually kills two options
✓ NO NEGATIVE MARKING — never leave a verbal question blank
```

## 10. Common Mistakes

| # | Mistake | Example | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Verb matched to the nearest noun** | The list of items **are** | Subject is "list" ⇒ **is** |
| 2 | **"Along with" treated like "and"** | The manager along with his team **were** | Parenthetical ⇒ **was** |
| 3 | **"The number of" made plural** | The number of students **are** | ⇒ **is** |
| 4 | **"Neither/each" made plural** | Neither of them **were** | ⇒ **was** |
| 5 | **"Since" used for a duration** | since five years | ⇒ **for** five years |
| 6 | **Stative verb in continuous form** | I have been **knowing** him | ⇒ have **known** |
| 7 | **Modal not backshifted** | He said he **will** come | ⇒ **would** come |
| 8 | **"Than" after a Latin comparative** | senior **than** me | ⇒ senior **to** me |
| 9 | **"One of the" + singular noun** | one of my **friend** | ⇒ one of my **friends** |
| 10 | **"to not" instead of "not to"** | asked them **to not** talk | ⇒ **not to** talk |
| 11 | **Article chosen by spelling** | **an** university | ⇒ **a** university (yoo sound) |
| 12 | **Dangling modifier** | Walking down the street, the building… | Put the actor after the comma |
| 13 | **Synonym chosen in antonym questions** | FRUGAL ⇒ thrifty | Thrifty is a synonym; the answer is extravagant |
| 14 | **Confusable pair mixed up** | archived / achieved | Check the meaning, not the spelling |
| 15 | **Jumble started from a back-reference** | Opening with "It has transformed…" | Openers never begin with It/This/However |
